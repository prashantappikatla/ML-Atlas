## Overview

Actor-Critic algorithms represent a class of reinforcement learning methods that combine aspects of policy-based and value-based approaches. They employ two distinct components: an "actor" that learns a policy to select actions, and a "critic" that evaluates the quality of the actor's actions by estimating the value function. This synergistic interaction allows Actor-Critic methods to leverage the stability of value-based methods with the ability of policy-based methods to handle continuous action spaces, often leading to more efficient and stable learning.

## History

**1983** — Barto, Sutton, and Anderson introduced one of the earliest forms of Actor-Critic architecture for the pole-balancing problem, demonstrating its potential for control tasks.

**1998** — V. R. Konda and J. N. Tsitsiklis published a seminal paper, "Actor-Critic Algorithms," providing a formal theoretical foundation and convergence analysis for these methods in both continuous and discrete time.

**1999** — The Actor-Critic framework was popularized and extensively discussed in Sutton and Barto's influential textbook, "[Reinforcement Learning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reinforcement_learning): An Introduction," solidifying its place in the field.

**2016** — Mnih et al. introduced Asynchronous Advantage Actor-Critic (A3C), a highly efficient and robust variant that enabled parallel training and achieved state-of-the-art performance on various Atari games.

**2017** — Schulman et al. proposed Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), an advanced policy gradient method that can be viewed as an Actor-Critic algorithm, known for its strong performance and stability across many tasks.

## Mathematical Intuition

### Actor and Critic Roles

Actor-Critic methods maintain two parameterized function approximators: an actor and a critic. The actor, parameterized by $\theta$, learns a policy $\pi_\theta(a|s)$ that maps states to a probability distribution over actions. The critic, parameterized by $w$, learns a state-value function $V_w(s)$ or an action-value function $Q_w(s,a)$, which estimates the expected return from a given state or state-action pair.

$$
\pi_\theta(a|s)
$$

$$
V_w(s)
$$

$$
Q_w(s,a)
$$

### Policy Gradient Theorem

The actor's objective is to maximize the expected return $J(\theta) = E_{\pi_\theta}[\sum_{t=0}^\infty \gamma^t r_t]$. The policy gradient theorem provides a way to compute the gradient of this objective with respect to the policy parameters $\theta$:$$ \nabla_\theta J(\theta) = E_{\pi_\theta}[\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a|s) Q^{\pi_\theta}(s,a)] $$The term $Q^{\pi_\theta}(s,a)$ represents the true action-value function under policy $\pi_\theta$. In Actor-Critic, the critic provides an estimate for this value.

$$
J(\theta) = E_{\pi_\theta}[\sum_{t=0}^\infty \gamma^t r_t]
$$

$$
\nabla_\theta J(\theta) = E_{\pi_\theta}[\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a|s) Q^{\pi_\theta}(s,a)]
$$

$$
Q^{\pi_\theta}(s,a)
$$

### Advantage Function Estimation

To reduce variance in the policy gradient estimate, the advantage function $A^{\pi_\theta}(s,a) = Q^{\pi_\theta}(s,a) - V^{\pi_\theta}(s)$ is often used. The critic estimates $V_w(s)$, and the action-value $Q^{\pi_\theta}(s,a)$ can be approximated using the observed reward $r_t$ and the critic's estimate of the next state's value $V_w(s_{t+1})$. The temporal difference (TD) error, $\delta_t = r_t + \gamma V_w(s_{t+1}) - V_w(s_t)$, serves as a common estimate for the advantage function.

$$
A^{\pi_\theta}(s,a) = Q^{\pi_\theta}(s,a) - V^{\pi_\theta}(s)
$$

$$
\delta_t = r_t + \gamma V_w(s_{t+1}) - V_w(s_t)
$$

### Actor and Critic Updates

The actor's parameters $\theta$ are updated in the direction of the policy gradient, using the critic's estimate of the advantage (e.g., the TD error $\delta_t$):$$ \theta \leftarrow \theta + \alpha_\theta \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t|s_t) \delta_t $$The critic's parameters $w$ are updated to minimize the squared TD error, typically via [Stochastic Gradient Descent](/algorithms/meta-learning/optimization/stochastic-gradient-descent), to improve its value function estimate:$$ w \leftarrow w + \alpha_w \delta_t \nabla_w V_w(s_t) $$Here, $\alpha_\theta$ and $\alpha_w$ are the learning rates for the actor and critic, respectively.

$$
\theta \leftarrow \theta + \alpha_\theta \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t|s_t) \delta_t
$$

$$
w \leftarrow w + \alpha_w \delta_t \nabla_w V_w(s_t)
$$

## Algorithm Steps

1. Initialize the actor's policy network parameters $\theta$ and the critic's value network parameters $w$ randomly.
2. Choose learning rates $\alpha_\theta$, $\alpha_w$, and a discount factor $\gamma$.
3. For each episode, observe an initial state $s_0$ from the environment.
4. For each time step $t$ within the episode:
5. Sample an action $a_t$ from the actor's policy $\pi_\theta(a_t|s_t)$.
6. Execute action $a_t$ in the environment, observe reward $r_{t+1}$ and next state $s_{t+1}$.
7. Compute the Temporal Difference (TD) error $\delta_t = r_{t+1} + \gamma V_w(s_{t+1}) - V_w(s_t)$. If $s_{t+1}$ is a terminal state, $V_w(s_{t+1})$ is 0.
8. Update the critic's parameters $w$ using gradient descent to minimize the squared TD error: $w \leftarrow w + \alpha_w \delta_t \nabla_w V_w(s_t)$.
9. Update the actor's parameters $\theta$ using the TD error as an advantage estimate: $\theta \leftarrow \theta + \alpha_\theta \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t|s_t) \delta_t$.
10. Set $s_t \leftarrow s_{t+1}$ and repeat until the episode terminates.

## Parameters

| Parameter | Type | Default | Range / Options | Description |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `learning_rate_actor` | `float` | `0.0001` | > 0 | The learning rate used for updating the actor's policy network parameters. |

## Strengths

- Actor-Critic methods often achieve lower variance in policy gradient estimates compared to pure policy gradient methods by using the critic's value function as a baseline.
- They are capable of learning optimal policies in continuous action spaces, which is a significant advantage over many value-based methods.
- The critic provides a more stable and informative learning signal for the actor, potentially leading to faster convergence and improved sample efficiency.
- The separation of policy and value functions allows for more flexible network architectures and optimization techniques for each component.
- Many advanced and state-of-the-art reinforcement learning algorithms are built upon the Actor-Critic framework, demonstrating its robustness and adaptability.

## Weaknesses

- Actor-Critic algorithms can be highly sensitive to hyperparameter tuning, particularly the learning rates for both the actor and the critic networks.
- The convergence and stability of the algorithm are heavily dependent on the critic's ability to accurately estimate the value function, which can be challenging.
- They can suffer from instability if the critic's value estimates are poor or if the policy changes too rapidly, leading to oscillations or divergence.
- Maintaining and updating two separate function approximators (actor and critic) can increase computational complexity compared to simpler methods.
- The choice of function approximators and their architectures significantly impacts performance, requiring careful design and experimentation.

## Use Cases

### Robotics

Training robots to perform complex manipulation tasks, such as grasping objects or navigating intricate environments, by learning continuous control policies.

### Game Playing

Developing AI agents that can play various video games, including those with high-dimensional state and action spaces, often achieving superhuman performance.

### Resource Management

Optimizing resource allocation in complex systems like data centers, communication networks, or energy grids to maximize efficiency and minimize costs.

### Autonomous Driving

Learning optimal control policies for self-driving cars, enabling them to make decisions in dynamic traffic scenarios and navigate safely.

### Financial Trading

Creating intelligent agents that can make optimal buy/sell decisions in dynamic and uncertain financial markets to maximize returns.

### Industrial Control

Optimizing control strategies for industrial processes, such as chemical reactions or manufacturing lines, to improve efficiency and product quality.
