## Overview

[SARSA](/algorithms/reinforcement/sarsa) (State-Action-Reward-State-Action) is an on-policy temporal difference control algorithm used in reinforcement learning to learn an optimal policy. It directly learns the action-value function $Q(s, a)$ for the policy it is currently following, making its updates dependent on the next action chosen by that same policy. This contrasts with off-policy methods like [Q-Learning](/algorithms/reinforcement/q-learning), which learn the optimal Q-function independently of the agent's current exploration strategy. SARSA is particularly well-suited for environments where the agent's exploration strategy directly impacts the learning process and safety is a significant concern.

## History

**1994** — Gavin Rummery and Mahesan Niranjan introduced the foundational algorithm in their paper "On-line Q-Learning using connectionist systems," laying the groundwork for on-policy temporal difference control.

**1998** — Richard S. Sutton and Andrew G. Barto formally named the algorithm SARSA in their seminal textbook "[Reinforcement Learning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reinforcement_learning): An Introduction," distinguishing it from Q-Learning and popularizing its use.

**2000** — SARSA was widely adopted and studied in academic research for various control problems, particularly in robotics and autonomous agents, due to its on-policy nature.

**2010** — The principles of SARSA were extended to handle high-dimensional state spaces through function approximation, leading to variants like Deep SARSA, although Deep Q-Networks (DQNs) gained more prominence in deep reinforcement learning.

## Mathematical Intuition

### SARSA Temporal Difference Update

The core of SARSA is its temporal difference (TD) update rule, which adjusts the action-value function $Q(S_t, A_t)$ based on the reward received and the estimated value of the next state-action pair. Unlike Q-Learning, the next action $A_{t+1}$ is chosen according to the *current policy* being followed by the agent. The update rule is given by:
$$Q(S_t, A_t) \leftarrow Q(S_t, A_t) + \alpha [R_{t+1} + \gamma Q(S_{t+1}, A_{t+1}) - Q(S_t, A_t)]$$
Here, $S_t$ is the current state, $A_t$ is the action taken, $R_{t+1}$ is the reward received, $S_{t+1}$ is the next state, and $A_{t+1}$ is the next action chosen by the same policy. $\alpha$ is the learning rate, and $\gamma$ is the discount factor. The term $R_{t+1} + \gamma Q(S_{t+1}, A_{t+1})$ represents the target value, and the difference with $Q(S_t, A_t)$ is the TD error.

$$
Q(S_t, A_t) \leftarrow Q(S_t, A_t) + \alpha [R_{t+1} + \gamma Q(S_{t+1}, A_{t+1}) - Q(S_t, A_t)]
$$

### On-Policy Learning Principle

SARSA is an on-policy algorithm, meaning it learns the value function for the policy currently being followed by the agent. Both the action $A_t$ and the subsequent action $A_{t+1}$ are selected using the same policy, typically an $\epsilon$-greedy policy derived from the current $Q$-values. This characteristic makes SARSA more cautious in environments where taking suboptimal actions can incur significant penalties, as it directly evaluates the consequences of its own exploration strategy. The learned $Q$-function thus represents the expected return of following the current (possibly exploratory) policy.

### Epsilon-Greedy Action Selection

To balance exploration and exploitation, SARSA commonly employs an $\epsilon$-greedy policy for action selection. With probability $\epsilon$, the agent chooses a random action to explore the environment. With probability $1 - \epsilon$, the agent exploits its current knowledge by choosing the action $a$ that maximizes $Q(s, a)$. This policy ensures that the agent continues to discover new state-action values while also leveraging its learned knowledge to achieve higher rewards. The probability distribution for choosing an action $a$ in state $s$ under an $\epsilon$-greedy policy is:
$$\pi(a|s) = \begin{cases} 1 - \epsilon + \epsilon/|\mathcal{A}(s)| & \text{if } a = \arg\max_{a'} Q(s, a') \\ \epsilon/|\mathcal{A}(s)| & \text{if } a \neq \arg\max_{a'} Q(s, a') \end{cases}$$
where $|\mathcal{A}(s)|$ is the number of available actions in state $s$. The value of $\epsilon$ is often decayed over time to shift from more exploration to more exploitation.

$$
\pi(a|s) = \begin{cases} 1 - \epsilon + \epsilon/|\mathcal{A}(s)| & \text{if } a = \arg\max_{a'} Q(s, a') \\ \epsilon/|\mathcal{A}(s)| & \text{if } a \neq \arg\max_{a'} Q(s, a') \end{cases}
$$

## Algorithm Steps

1. Initialize $Q(s, a)$ for all state-action pairs arbitrarily (e.g., to zero), and $Q(\text{terminal state}, \cdot) = 0$.
2. Set hyperparameters: learning rate $\alpha \in (0, 1]$, discount factor $\gamma \in [0, 1]$, and exploration rate $\epsilon \in [0, 1]$.
3. For each episode, initialize the starting state $S$.
4. Choose an initial action $A$ from $S$ using an $\epsilon$-greedy policy derived from the current $Q$-values.
5. For each step of the episode, while $S$ is not a terminal state:
6. Take action $A$, observe the immediate reward $R$ and the next state $S'$.
7. Choose the next action $A'$ from $S'$ using the same $\epsilon$-greedy policy derived from $Q$.
8. Update the action-value function: $Q(S, A) \leftarrow Q(S, A) + \alpha [R + \gamma Q(S', A') - Q(S, A)]$.
9. Set $S \leftarrow S'$ and $A \leftarrow A'$ for the next iteration.

## Parameters

| Parameter | Type | Default | Range / Options | Description |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `alpha` | `float` | `0.1` | (0, 1] | The learning rate, determining the extent to which the Q-values are updated based on new information. |
| `gamma` | `float` | `0.99` | [0, 1] | The discount factor, which determines the importance of future rewards. A higher value emphasizes long-term rewards. |
| `epsilon` | `float` | `0.1` | [0, 1] | The exploration rate, controlling the probability of choosing a random action instead of the greedy action. |
| `n_episodes` | `int` | `1000` | >= 1 | The total number of episodes to run the learning process. |
| `epsilon_decay` | `float` | `0.995` | (0, 1] | The decay rate for epsilon per episode, gradually reducing exploration over time. |
| `min_epsilon` | `float` | `0.01` | [0, 1] | The minimum value epsilon can decay to, ensuring some level of continuous exploration. |

## Strengths

- SARSA is an on-policy algorithm, making it suitable for learning optimal policies in environments where the agent's exploration strategy directly impacts safety or cost.
- It converges to an optimal policy if all state-action pairs are visited infinitely often and the policy converges to a greedy policy (e.g., by decaying $\epsilon$).
- The algorithm is relatively simple to implement for environments with discrete state and action spaces.
- SARSA's on-policy nature can lead to safer exploration strategies compared to off-policy methods in certain real-world scenarios.
- It provides a direct estimate of the value of taking a specific action in a given state under the current policy.

## Weaknesses

- SARSA can be less efficient in terms of sample complexity compared to off-policy methods like Q-learning, as it only learns from experiences generated by its current policy.
- It struggles with large or continuous state and action spaces, requiring function approximation techniques to scale effectively.
- The algorithm's performance is highly sensitive to the choice of hyperparameters, particularly the learning rate $\alpha$ and exploration rate $\epsilon$.
- Finding an optimal balance between exploration and exploitation can be challenging, especially in complex environments with sparse rewards.
- SARSA's on-policy nature means it might learn a suboptimal policy if the exploration strategy is not sufficiently diverse or if the environment has "cliffs" where exploration leads to severe penalties.

## Use Cases

### Robotics

Training robots to perform tasks like navigation or manipulation where safety during the learning process is critical, as SARSA's on-policy nature avoids exploring dangerous paths.

### Game Playing

Developing agents for simple board games or video games with discrete states and actions, where the agent learns to optimize its strategy through direct interaction.

### Resource Management

Optimizing resource allocation in systems where actions have immediate and observable consequences, such as managing energy consumption or network bandwidth.

### Traffic Control

Designing adaptive traffic light systems to minimize congestion based on real-time traffic flow, where the policy's actions directly influence the environment it learns from.

### Healthcare

Personalizing treatment plans or drug dosages based on patient responses, considering the on-policy nature for safety and ethical considerations in medical applications.
