## Overview

[BERT](/algorithms/deep-learning/bert) (Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers) is a powerful pre-training framework designed to learn deep bidirectional representations from unlabeled text by jointly conditioning on both left and right context in all layers. It revolutionized natural language processing by enabling state-of-the-art performance across a wide range of downstream tasks with minimal task-specific architecture modifications. Its core innovation lies in its novel pre-training objectives: Masked Language Model and Next Sentence Prediction.

## History

**2017** — Vaswani et al. introduced the [Transformer](/algorithms/deep-learning/transformer) architecture, relying solely on attention mechanisms, which became the foundational component for many subsequent NLP models.

**2018** — ELMo (Embeddings from Language Models) by Peters et al. demonstrated the effectiveness of deep contextualized word representations learned from bidirectional LSTMs.

**2018** — OpenAI's [GPT](/algorithms/deep-learning/gpt) (Generative Pre-trained Transformer) by Radford et al. showed the power of generative pre-training using a Transformer decoder for various NLP tasks.

**2018** — Devlin et al. introduced BERT, proposing a bidirectional Transformer encoder pre-trained with Masked Language Modeling and Next Sentence Prediction objectives.

**2019** — [RoBERTa](/algorithms/deep-learning/roberta) (Liu et al.) improved BERT's pre-training by optimizing hyperparameters, removing the Next Sentence Prediction task, and training on more data for better performance.

**2019** — [ALBERT](/algorithms/deep-learning/albert) (Lan et al.) introduced parameter-sharing techniques to significantly reduce BERT's memory footprint and increase its training speed.

## Mathematical Intuition

### Transformer Encoder Architecture

The core of BERT is the Transformer encoder, which processes input sequences using multi-head self-attention mechanisms. Each encoder layer consists of a multi-head self-attention sub-layer and a position-wise feed-forward network. The self-[Attention Mechanism](/algorithms/deep-learning/attention-mechanism) computes a weighted sum of value vectors based on query-key similarity, allowing the model to weigh the importance of different words in the input sequence.
$$Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax(\frac{QK^T}{\sqrt{d_k}})V$$
Here, $Q$, $K$, and $V$ are the query, key, and value matrices, respectively, derived from the input embeddings, and $d_k$ is the dimension of the key vectors. Multi-head attention performs this operation multiple times in parallel with different linear projections, then concatenates and linearly transforms their outputs.

$$
Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax(\frac{QK^T}{\sqrt{d_k}})V
$$

### Masked Language Modeling (MLM)

The MLM objective randomly masks a percentage of input tokens and then predicts the original vocabulary ID of the masked tokens based on their context. This forces the model to learn bidirectional representations by integrating information from both left and right contexts. The loss function for MLM is typically a cross-entropy loss over the masked tokens.
$$L_{MLM} = -\sum_{i \in M} \log P(w_i | \text{context of } w_i)$$
where $M$ is the set of masked token positions, $w_i$ is the original token at position $i$, and $P(w_i | \text{context of } w_i)$ is the predicted probability of the original token.

$$
L_{MLM} = -\sum_{i \in M} \log P(w_i | \text{context of } w_i)
$$

### Next Sentence Prediction (NSP)

The NSP objective trains the model to understand the relationship between two sentences. For each pre-training example, two sentences, A and B, are chosen. 50% of the time, B is the actual next sentence that follows A, and 50% of the time, B is a random sentence from the corpus. The model then predicts whether B is indeed the next sentence using a binary classifier. This helps BERT learn sentence-level representations crucial for tasks like question answering and natural language inference.
$$L_{NSP} = -\left[ y \log(\hat{y}) + (1-y) \log(1-\hat{y}) \right]$$
where $y$ is the true label (IsNext or NotNext) and $\hat{y}$ is the predicted probability.

$$
L_{NSP} = -\left[ y \log(\hat{y}) + (1-y) \log(1-\hat{y}) \right]
$$

### Input Representation and Embeddings

BERT's input representation for a given token is the sum of three embeddings: the token embedding, the segment embedding (indicating whether the token belongs to sentence A or B), and the positional embedding. Positional embeddings are crucial because Transformers do not inherently process sequence order. They add information about the absolute or relative position of tokens in the sequence.
$$E_{input} = E_{token} + E_{segment} + E_{position}$$
These embeddings are learned during pre-training, allowing the model to distinguish between tokens at different positions and in different segments.

$$
E_{input} = E_{token} + E_{segment} + E_{position}
$$

## Algorithm Steps

1. Tokenize the input text into WordPiece tokens and construct input sequences by concatenating sentence A and sentence B, separated by a special $[SEP]$ token, and starting with a $[CLS]$ token.
2. For each token, generate its token embedding, segment embedding (indicating sentence A or B), and positional embedding, then sum these three embeddings to form the final input representation.
3. Randomly select 15% of the tokens in each input sequence for masking; of these selected tokens, 80% are replaced with $[MASK]$, 10% are replaced with a random token, and 10% remain unchanged.
4. For each input sequence, pair sentence A with sentence B, where with 50% probability B is the actual next sentence to A (IsNext), and with 50% probability B is a random sentence (NotNext).
5. Feed the combined input embeddings through a multi-layer Transformer encoder, which applies multi-head self-attention and feed-forward networks to produce contextualized representations for each token.
6. Apply a prediction head (a feed-forward network followed by a softmax) on the output representation corresponding to the masked tokens to predict their original vocabulary IDs.
7. Apply a prediction head (a feed-forward network followed by a softmax) on the output representation of the $[CLS]$ token to predict whether sentence B is the next sentence to A.
8. Calculate the combined loss as the sum of the Masked Language Model loss and the Next Sentence Prediction loss, and update model parameters using an optimizer (e.g., [Adam](/algorithms/meta-learning/adam)) to minimize this joint loss.

## Strengths

- BERT learns deep, bidirectional contextual representations, allowing it to understand the full context of a word based on all its surrounding words.
- Its pre-training on large text corpora enables strong transfer learning capabilities, achieving state-of-the-art results on many downstream NLP tasks with minimal fine-tuning.
- The Transformer architecture's parallelizability allows for efficient training on modern hardware compared to recurrent neural networks.
- It provides a unified framework for various NLP tasks, reducing the need for task-specific architectures.
- The Masked Language Model objective effectively trains the model to predict missing words, leading to a rich understanding of language semantics.
- The Next Sentence Prediction task helps the model learn relationships between sentences, which is crucial for tasks requiring discourse understanding.

## Weaknesses

- BERT models are computationally expensive to pre-train, requiring significant GPU resources and time.
- The large number of parameters in BERT models can lead to high memory consumption, making deployment challenging on resource-constrained devices.
- The fixed vocabulary used by BERT can struggle with out-of-vocabulary words or highly specialized domain-specific terminology.
- The 'masking' strategy during pre-training creates a discrepancy between pre-training and fine-tuning, where the $[MASK]$ token is not present in fine-tuning.
- BERT's pre-training data can contain biases present in the internet text, which can be reflected in the model's predictions.

## Use Cases

### Text Classification

Classifying documents or sentences into predefined categories, such as sentiment analysis or spam detection.

### Question Answering

Answering questions based on a given text passage by identifying the span of text that contains the answer.

### Named Entity Recognition

Identifying and classifying named entities (e.g., persons, organizations, locations) in text.

### Text Summarization

Generating concise summaries of longer documents, either abstractive or extractive.

### Machine Translation

Enhancing the quality of translations by providing rich contextual embeddings for source and target languages.

### Information Retrieval

Improving search relevance by understanding the semantic meaning of queries and documents.
