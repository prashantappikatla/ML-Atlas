## Overview

Restricted Boltzmann Machines (RBMs) are generative stochastic artificial neural networks that learn a probability distribution over their set of inputs. They are a type of [Boltzmann Machine](/algorithms/deep-learning/other-architectures/boltzmann-machine) restricted to a two-layer architecture with no intra-layer connections, which makes inference tractable. RBMs are often used for dimensionality reduction, feature learning, and collaborative filtering, and served as fundamental building blocks for deep belief networks.

## History

**1986** — Paul Smolensky introduced the [Boltzmann Machine](/algorithms/deep-learning/other-architectures/boltzmann-machine), a stochastic [Recurrent Neural Network](/algorithms/deep-learning/sequence-models/recurrent-neural-network), laying the groundwork for energy-based models.

**1986** — Geoffrey Hinton and Terrence Sejnowski further developed Boltzmann Machines, demonstrating their ability to learn complex patterns through a global energy function.

**2002** — Geoffrey Hinton introduced the 'Restricted [Boltzmann Machine](/algorithms/deep-learning/other-architectures/boltzmann-machine)' as a more computationally efficient variant of the [Boltzmann Machine](/algorithms/deep-learning/other-architectures/boltzmann-machine), simplifying the architecture by removing connections within layers.

**2006** — Geoffrey Hinton and Ruslan Salakhutdinov showed that RBMs could be effectively trained using Contrastive Divergence, enabling their use as building blocks for Deep Belief Networks and pre-training deep neural networks.

**2007** — RBMs gained significant attention for their application in collaborative filtering, particularly in the Netflix Prize competition, where they were used for recommendation systems.

## Mathematical Intuition

### Energy Function Definition

The core of an RBM is its energy function, which quantifies the 'goodness' of a configuration of visible and hidden units. For a given visible vector $v$ and hidden vector $h$, the energy $E(v, h)$ is defined as:
$$E(v, h) = -\sum_{i=1}^{N_v} a_i v_i - \sum_{j=1}^{N_h} b_j h_j - \sum_{i=1}^{N_v} \sum_{j=1}^{N_h} v_i W_{ij} h_j$$
where $v_i$ are the states of visible units, $h_j$ are the states of hidden units, $a_i$ are the biases for visible units, $b_j$ are the biases for hidden units, and $W_{ij}$ are the symmetric weights connecting visible unit $i$ to hidden unit $j$.

$$
E(v, h) = -\sum_{i=1}^{N_v} a_i v_i - \sum_{j=1}^{N_h} b_j h_j - \sum_{i=1}^{N_v} \sum_{j=1}^{N_h} v_i W_{ij} h_j
$$

### Probability Distribution over States

The probability of a joint configuration $(v, h)$ is determined by the energy function using a Boltzmann distribution:
$$P(v, h) = \frac{1}{Z} e^{-E(v, h)}$$
where $Z$ is the partition function, a normalization constant that sums over all possible configurations of visible and hidden units:
$$Z = \sum_{v, h} e^{-E(v, h)}$$
The marginal probability of a visible vector $v$ is obtained by summing over all possible hidden configurations:
$$P(v) = \frac{1}{Z} \sum_h e^{-E(v, h)}$$

$$
P(v, h) = \frac{1}{Z} e^{-E(v, h)}
$$

$$
Z = \sum_{v, h} e^{-E(v, h)}
$$

$$
P(v) = \frac{1}{Z} \sum_h e^{-E(v, h)}
$$

### Conditional Probabilities

Due to the bipartite graph structure (no intra-layer connections), the states of visible units are conditionally independent given the hidden units, and vice versa. This allows for efficient computation of conditional probabilities:
$$P(h_j=1|v) = \sigma \left( b_j + \sum_{i=1}^{N_v} W_{ij} v_i \right)$$
$$P(v_i=1|h) = \sigma \left( a_i + \sum_{j=1}^{N_h} W_{ij} h_j \right)$$
where $\sigma(x) = 1 / (1 + e^{-x})$ is the logistic sigmoid function. These conditional probabilities are crucial for Gibbs sampling during training.

$$
P(h_j=1|v) = \sigma \left( b_j + \sum_{i=1}^{N_v} W_{ij} v_i \right)
$$

$$
P(v_i=1|h) = \sigma \left( a_i + \sum_{j=1}^{N_h} W_{ij} h_j \right)
$$

$$
\sigma(x) = 1 / (1 + e^{-x})
$$

### Learning with Contrastive Divergence

RBMs are typically trained to maximize the log-likelihood of the training data using gradient descent. The gradient of the log-likelihood with respect to a weight $W_{ij}$ is given by:
$$\frac{\partial \log P(v)}{\partial W_{ij}} = \langle v_i h_j \rangle_{\text{data}} - \langle v_i h_j \rangle_{\text{model}}$$
where $\langle \cdot \rangle_{\text{data}}$ is the expectation with respect to the data distribution $P(v)$ and $\langle \cdot \rangle_{\text{model}}$ is the expectation with respect to the model's distribution $P(v, h)$. Contrastive Divergence (CD-k) approximates the intractable $\langle v_i h_j \rangle_{\text{model}}$ term by performing $k$ steps of Gibbs sampling starting from a training example $v^{(0)}$. The update rule for weights is:
$$\Delta W_{ij} = \eta (\langle v_i h_j \rangle_{\text{data}} - \langle v_i h_j \rangle_{\text{reconstruction}})$$
where $\eta$ is the learning rate, $\langle v_i h_j \rangle_{\text{data}}$ is computed from the initial data sample, and $\langle v_i h_j \rangle_{\text{reconstruction}}$ is computed from the sample after $k$ Gibbs steps.

$$
\frac{\partial \log P(v)}{\partial W_{ij}} = \langle v_i h_j \rangle_{\text{data}} - \langle v_i h_j \rangle_{\text{model}}
$$

$$
\Delta W_{ij} = \eta (\langle v_i h_j \rangle_{\text{data}} - \langle v_i h_j \rangle_{\text{reconstruction}})
$$

## Algorithm Steps

1. Initialize weights $W$, visible biases $a$, and hidden biases $b$ randomly.
2. For each training example $v^{(0)}$:
3. Compute the probabilities of activating hidden units given $v^{(0)}$: $P(h_j=1|v^{(0)}) = \sigma(b_j + \sum_i W_{ij} v_i^{(0)})$.
4. Sample a hidden vector $h^{(0)}$ from these probabilities.
5. Compute the probabilities of activating visible units given $h^{(0)}$: $P(v_i=1|h^{(0)}) = \sigma(a_i + \sum_j W_{ij} h_j^{(0)})$.
6. Sample a reconstructed visible vector $v^{(1)}$ from these probabilities.
7. Compute the probabilities of activating hidden units given $v^{(1)}$: $P(h_j=1|v^{(1)}) = \sigma(b_j + \sum_i W_{ij} v_i^{(1)})$.
8. Update weights and biases using the Contrastive Divergence gradient: $\Delta W_{ij} = \eta (v_i^{(0)} P(h_j=1|v^{(0)}) - v_i^{(1)} P(h_j=1|v^{(1)}))$, and similarly for biases.

## Strengths

- RBMs are effective feature learners, capable of extracting hierarchical representations from raw data.
- They can be stacked to form Deep Belief Networks, enabling the pre-training of deep neural networks layer by layer.
- RBMs are generative models, meaning they can learn the underlying probability distribution of the input data and generate new samples.
- The bipartite structure allows for efficient computation of conditional probabilities $P(h|v)$ and $P(v|h)$.
- They are useful for dimensionality reduction, projecting high-dimensional data into a lower-dimensional hidden representation.
- RBMs can handle missing data naturally during reconstruction, making them suitable for collaborative filtering tasks.

## Weaknesses

- Training RBMs with Contrastive Divergence is an approximation and does not guarantee convergence to the true log-likelihood gradient.
- Determining the optimal number of hidden units and other hyperparameters can be challenging and often requires extensive tuning.
- RBMs can be computationally intensive to train on very large datasets, especially with many hidden units.
- The interpretation of learned features in the hidden layer can be difficult due to their abstract nature.
- They are sensitive to initialization and learning rate, which can affect the quality of the learned representations.

## Use Cases

### Recommendation Systems

Used in collaborative filtering to predict user preferences for items based on past interactions.

### Image Recognition

Applied for feature extraction and dimensionality reduction in image datasets, often as part of a larger deep learning architecture.

### Natural Language Processing

Employed for learning word embeddings or features from text data, useful for tasks like topic modeling or sentiment analysis.

### Anomaly Detection

Can learn the normal distribution of data and identify outliers as samples with low reconstruction probability.

### Medical Imaging

Used for feature learning from medical scans to aid in diagnosis or disease detection.

### Speech Recognition

Applied for pre-training acoustic models by learning robust features from raw audio spectrograms.
