## Overview

The Boltzmann Machine is a stochastic [Recurrent Neural Network](/algorithms/deep-learning/sequence-models/recurrent-neural-network) that learns to represent complex probability distributions over its input data. It is an energy-based model, where the probability of a given configuration of units is inversely proportional to its energy, allowing it to model highly non-linear relationships. This unsupervised learning algorithm aims to find a set of weights that minimizes the global energy of "good" configurations and maximizes the energy of "bad" configurations, effectively learning a generative model of the data.

## History

**1985** — Geoffrey Hinton, Terrence Sejnowski, and David Ackley introduced the Boltzmann Machine as a stochastic [Recurrent Neural Network](/algorithms/deep-learning/sequence-models/recurrent-neural-network) capable of learning internal representations and solving optimization problems.

**1986** — The widespread adoption of backpropagation for feedforward networks, popularized by Rumelhart, Hinton, and Williams, temporarily overshadowed Boltzmann Machines due to their computational complexity.

**2002** — Geoffrey Hinton and Simon Osindero introduced Contrastive Divergence (CD) as an efficient approximation for training Restricted Boltzmann Machines (RBMs), a simpler variant of the Boltzmann Machine.

**2006** — Hinton and Ruslan Salakhutdinov demonstrated how to stack RBMs to form Deep Belief Networks (DBNs), reigniting interest in generative deep learning and pre-training deep networks.

## Mathematical Intuition

### Energy Function of a Configuration

The Boltzmann Machine defines an energy function $E(\mathbf{v}, \mathbf{h})$ for a given configuration of visible units $\mathbf{v}$ and hidden units $\mathbf{h}$. This function quantifies how "good" or "bad" a particular state is, with lower energy indicating more probable states. The general form for a fully connected Boltzmann Machine is:
$$E(\mathbf{v}, \mathbf{h}) = -\sum_{i \in V} b_i v_i - \sum_{j \in H} b_j h_j - \sum_{i \in V} \sum_{j \in H} w_{ij} v_i h_j - \sum_{i,k \in V, i<k} w_{ik} v_i v_k - \sum_{j,l \in H, j<l} w_{jl} h_j h_l$$
where $V$ is the set of visible units, $H$ is the set of hidden units, $v_i$ and $h_j$ are the binary states of units, $b_i$ and $b_j$ are their biases, and $w_{ij}$ are the symmetric connection weights between units. The original Boltzmann Machine allows connections between all units, including within visible and hidden layers.

$$
E(\mathbf{v}, \mathbf{h}) = -\sum_{i \in V} b_i v_i - \sum_{j \in H} b_j h_j - \sum_{i \in V} \sum_{j \in H} w_{ij} v_i h_j - \sum_{i,k \in V, i<k} w_{ik} v_i v_k - \sum_{j,l \in H, j<l} w_{jl} h_j h_l
$$

### Probability Distribution over States

The probability of a global configuration $(\mathbf{v}, \mathbf{h})$ is given by the Boltzmann distribution, which relates the energy of a state to its likelihood. The probability is proportional to the negative exponential of its energy, normalized by a partition function $Z$.
$$P(\mathbf{v}, \mathbf{h}) = \frac{e^{-E(\mathbf{v}, \mathbf{h})}}{Z}$$
where $Z = \sum_{\mathbf{v}, \mathbf{h}} e^{-E(\mathbf{v}, \mathbf{h})}$ is the partition function, summing over all possible configurations of visible and hidden units. The goal of training is to adjust weights and biases such that the model's probability distribution $P(\mathbf{v})$ matches the empirical distribution of the training data.

$$
P(\mathbf{v}, \mathbf{h}) = \frac{e^{-E(\mathbf{v}, \mathbf{h})}}{Z}
$$

$$
Z = \sum_{\mathbf{v}, \mathbf{h}} e^{-E(\mathbf{v}, \mathbf{h})}
$$

### Stochastic Unit Activation

Units in a Boltzmann Machine are binary (0 or 1) and stochastic. The probability of a unit $u_k$ being active (i.e., $u_k=1$) given the states of all other units $\mathbf{u}_{\neg k}$ is determined by a logistic sigmoid function of its net input.
$$P(u_k=1 | \mathbf{u}_{\neg k}) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-\text{net_input}_k}}$$
where $\text{net_input}_k = b_k + \sum_{j \neq k} w_{kj} u_j$. This stochastic activation allows the network to escape local minima and explore the energy landscape, crucial for learning complex distributions.

$$
P(u_k=1 | \mathbf{u}_{\neg k}) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-\text{net_input}_k}}
$$

$$
\text{net_input}_k = b_k + \sum_{j \neq k} w_{kj} u_j
$$

### Learning Rule for Weights

Training a Boltzmann Machine involves adjusting the weights $w_{ij}$ and biases $b_i$ to maximize the likelihood of the training data. This is typically done using gradient ascent on the log-likelihood. The gradient for a weight $w_{ij}$ is given by:
$$\frac{\partial \log P(\mathbf{v})}{\partial w_{ij}} = \langle v_i h_j \rangle_{data} - \langle v_i h_j \rangle_{model}$$
where $\langle \cdot \rangle_{data}$ denotes the expectation with respect to the data distribution (positive phase) and $\langle \cdot \rangle_{model}$ denotes the expectation with respect to the model's distribution (negative phase). The learning rule updates weights by $w_{ij} \leftarrow w_{ij} + \eta (\langle v_i h_j \rangle_{data} - \langle v_i h_j \rangle_{model})$, where $\eta$ is the learning rate. Calculating $\langle v_i h_j \rangle_{model}$ requires extensive Gibbs sampling to reach thermal equilibrium, which is computationally expensive.

$$
\frac{\partial \log P(\mathbf{v})}{\partial w_{ij}} = \langle v_i h_j \rangle_{data} - \langle v_i h_j \rangle_{model}
$$

$$
w_{ij} \leftarrow w_{ij} + \eta (\langle v_i h_j \rangle_{data} - \langle v_i h_j \rangle_{model})
$$

## Algorithm Steps

1. Initialize all connection weights $w_{ij}$ and biases $b_i$ to small random values.
2. For each training example $\mathbf{v}_{data}$ or batch of examples, perform the positive phase:
3. Clamp the visible units to $\mathbf{v}_{data}$ and perform Gibbs sampling by iteratively updating the states of hidden units until the network reaches thermal equilibrium, then compute the pairwise product expectations $\langle v_i h_j \rangle_{data}$ and other relevant correlations.
4. Perform the negative phase by starting with a random configuration or the equilibrium state from the positive phase.
5. Perform Gibbs sampling for a sufficient number of steps, allowing both visible and hidden units to update freely, to reach thermal equilibrium, then compute the pairwise product expectations $\langle v_i h_j \rangle_{model}$ and other relevant correlations.
6. Update the weights and biases using the learning rule: $\Delta w_{ij} = \eta (\langle v_i h_j \rangle_{data} - \langle v_i h_j \rangle_{model})$, and similarly for biases and within-layer connections.
7. Repeat the positive and negative phases and weight updates for a specified number of epochs or until convergence.

## Parameters

| Parameter | Type | Default | Range / Options | Description |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `n_visible_units` | `int` | `None` | > 0 | Number of visible units, corresponding to the dimensionality of the input data. |
| `n_hidden_units` | `int` | `None` | > 0 | Number of hidden units, which learn to represent the latent features of the data. |
| `learning_rate` | `float` | `0.01` | > 0 | The step size for updating weights and biases during gradient ascent. |
| `n_epochs` | `int` | `100` | > 0 | The number of full passes over the training dataset. |
| `batch_size` | `int` | `1` | > 0 | The number of training examples utilized in one iteration of weight update. |
| `gibbs_steps_positive` | `int` | `1` | > 0 | Number of Gibbs sampling steps for the positive phase to reach equilibrium. Often set to 1 for RBMs, but higher for full BMs. |
| `gibbs_steps_negative` | `int` | `100` | > 0 | Number of Gibbs sampling steps for the negative phase to reach equilibrium. This typically requires many steps. |
| `weight_decay` | `float` | `0.0` | >= 0.0 | L2 regularization term to prevent overfitting by penalizing large weights. |
| `random_state` | `int or None` | `None` | integer or None | Seed for the random number generator for reproducibility. |

## Strengths

- Boltzmann Machines are capable of learning complex, high-order statistical dependencies within data, making them powerful generative models.
- They can perform unsupervised feature learning, extracting meaningful representations from raw input data without explicit labels.
- The stochastic nature of the units allows the network to escape local minima during training and explore a wider range of possible configurations.
- Boltzmann Machines can naturally handle missing data by treating unknown values as unclamped units during inference.
- They provide a principled probabilistic framework for modeling data distributions, offering insights into the underlying data generation process.

## Weaknesses

- Training the original Boltzmann Machine is computationally very expensive due to the need for extensive Gibbs sampling to reach thermal equilibrium in both positive and negative phases.
- The convergence to equilibrium can be extremely slow, especially for large networks, making practical application challenging.
- Calculating the partition function $Z$ is intractable for all but the smallest networks, necessitating approximations for likelihood estimation.
- The requirement for binary units limits direct application to continuous data without prior discretization or transformation.
- The full connectivity of the original Boltzmann Machine leads to a large number of parameters, increasing the risk of overfitting and computational burden.

## Use Cases

### Collaborative Filtering

Recommending items to users by learning patterns in user-item interactions, where visible units represent user ratings or preferences.

### Feature Learning

Extracting abstract and robust features from raw data, such as images or text, for use in subsequent supervised tasks like classification.

### Dimensionality Reduction

Learning a lower-dimensional representation of high-dimensional data by using the hidden layer as a compressed encoding, useful for visualization or preprocessing.

### Image Denoising

Reconstructing clean images from noisy inputs by learning the underlying clean image distribution and inferring the most probable clean state.

### Pattern Recognition

Identifying and classifying complex patterns in data, leveraging its ability to model intricate relationships and generalize from examples.

### Generative Modeling

Creating new data samples that resemble the training data, such as generating novel images, text sequences, or other data types.
