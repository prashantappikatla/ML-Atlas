## Overview

Variational Autoencoders (VAEs) are a class of generative models that learn a probabilistic mapping from data to a continuous latent space and back. Unlike traditional autoencoders, VAEs model the latent space as a probability distribution, enabling the generation of novel data samples by sampling from this learned distribution. This probabilistic approach allows VAEs to generate diverse outputs and provides a structured latent representation suitable for interpolation and other latent space manipulations.

## History

**2013** — Diederik P. Kingma and [Max Welling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_Welling) introduced the [Variational Autoencoder](/algorithms/generative/variational-autoencoder) in their seminal paper "Auto-Encoding Variational Bayes," establishing a new paradigm for deep generative models based on variational inference.

**2016** — Conditional Variational Autoencoders (CVAEs) were proposed, extending VAEs to generate data conditioned on specific attributes or labels.

**2017** — $eta$-VAEs were introduced to encourage disentangled representations in the latent space by weighting the KL divergence term in the ELBO objective.

**2018** — Adversarial Variational Bayes (AVB) combined VAEs with adversarial training to improve the quality of generated samples and address posterior collapse.

## Mathematical Intuition

### Variational Inference Foundation

VAEs leverage variational inference to approximate the intractable true posterior distribution $p(z|x)$ over latent variables $z$ given observed data $x$. Instead of directly computing $p(z|x)$, which often involves an intractable marginal likelihood $p(x)$, VAEs introduce an approximate posterior $q_\phi(z|x)$, typically a simple distribution like a Gaussian, parameterized by an encoder network with weights $\phi$. The goal is to minimize the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence between $q_\phi(z|x)$ and $p(z|x)$.

$$
D_{KL}(q_\phi(z|x) || p(z|x)) = \mathbb{E}_{q_\phi(z|x)}[\log q_\phi(z|x) - \log p(z|x)]
$$

### Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO)

Minimizing $D_{KL}(q_\phi(z|x) || p(z|x))$ is equivalent to maximizing the Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO) of the marginal likelihood $\log p(x)$. The ELBO objective function, which VAEs optimize, can be decomposed into two terms: a reconstruction term and a regularization term.
$$\log p(x) \ge \mathbb{E}_{q_\phi(z|x)}[\log p_\theta(x|z)] - D_{KL}(q_\phi(z|x) || p(z))$$
The first term, $\mathbb{E}_{q_\phi(z|x)}[\log p_\theta(x|z)]$, is the expected negative reconstruction error, encouraging the decoder $p_\theta(x|z)$ to accurately reconstruct the input $x$ from a latent sample $z$. The second term, $-D_{KL}(q_\phi(z|x) || p(z))$, acts as a regularizer, forcing the approximate posterior $q_\phi(z|x)$ to stay close to a prior distribution $p(z)$, typically a standard normal distribution.

$$
\log p(x) \ge \mathbb{E}_{q_\phi(z|x)}[\log p_\theta(x|z)] - D_{KL}(q_\phi(z|x) || p(z))
$$

### Reparameterization Trick for Gradients

To enable backpropagation through the sampling process from $q_\phi(z|x)$, the reparameterization trick is employed. If $q_\phi(z|x)$ is a Gaussian distribution with mean $\mu(x)$ and standard deviation $\sigma(x)$ (output by the encoder), a sample $z$ cannot be directly obtained in a differentiable manner. Instead, $z$ is sampled from a standard normal distribution $\mathcal{N}(0, I)$ and then transformed:
$$z = \mu(x) + \sigma(x) \odot \epsilon$$
where $\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$ is a random noise vector. This reparameterization separates the stochasticity from the network parameters $\phi$ and $\theta$, allowing gradients to flow through $\mu(x)$ and $\sigma(x)$ during training.

$$
z = \mu(x) + \sigma(x) \odot \epsilon
$$

## Algorithm Steps

1. An input data point $x$ is fed into the encoder network.
2. The encoder outputs the parameters (mean $\mu$ and log-variance $\log \sigma^2$) of the approximate posterior distribution $q_\phi(z|x)$ in the latent space.
3. A latent vector $z$ is sampled from this distribution using the reparameterization trick: $z = \mu + \sigma \odot \epsilon$, where $\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$.
4. The sampled latent vector $z$ is passed to the decoder network.
5. The decoder reconstructs the input data, producing an output $x'$.
6. The reconstruction loss (e.g., mean squared error for continuous data or binary cross-entropy for binary data) is calculated between $x$ and $x'$.
7. The Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence between the approximate posterior $q_\phi(z|x)$ and the prior $p(z)$ (typically $\mathcal{N}(0, I)$) is calculated.
8. The total loss, the negative ELBO, is computed as the sum of the reconstruction loss and the KL divergence term.
9. The network parameters (encoder $\phi$ and decoder $\theta$) are updated via backpropagation to minimize this total loss.

## Parameters

| Parameter | Type | Default | Range / Options | Description |
|---|---|---|---|---|

## Strengths

- VAEs provide a continuous and structured latent space, enabling smooth interpolation between data points.
- They offer a probabilistic framework for generative modeling, allowing for uncertainty quantification in the latent representations.
- The reparameterization trick allows for end-to-end training using gradient-based optimization methods.
- VAEs are less prone to mode collapse compared to [Generative Adversarial Networks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generative_adversarial_network), as they optimize a lower bound on the data likelihood.
- The learned latent space can be used for various downstream tasks such as anomaly detection and dimensionality reduction.
- They can generate diverse and novel samples that resemble the training data distribution.

## Weaknesses

- Generated samples from VAEs often appear blurry or less sharp than those produced by Generative Adversarial Networks.
- The Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO) is a lower bound on the true log-likelihood, meaning the model might not fully capture the data distribution.
- VAEs can suffer from "posterior collapse," where the approximate posterior $q_\phi(z|x)$ collapses to the prior $p(z)$, making the latent variables uninformative.
- Tuning the weight of the KL divergence term (e.g., in $\beta$-VAEs) can be challenging and significantly impact the quality of generation and disentanglement.
- The assumption of a simple prior distribution (e.g., Gaussian) might limit the expressiveness of the latent space for complex data distributions.

## Use Cases

### Image Generation

Creating novel images, such as faces or objects, by sampling from the learned latent space.

### Anomaly Detection

Identifying unusual data points by measuring their reconstruction error or their position in the latent space.

### Data Imputation

Filling in missing values in datasets by leveraging the learned data distribution and latent representations.

### Drug Discovery

Generating new molecular structures with desired properties by exploring the latent space of chemical compounds.

### Text Generation

Producing new sentences or documents that maintain semantic coherence and style.

### Dimensionality Reduction

Learning meaningful low-dimensional representations of high-dimensional data for visualization or further analysis.
