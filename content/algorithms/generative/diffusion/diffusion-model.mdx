## Overview

Diffusion Models are a class of generative models that learn to reverse a gradual noising process, transforming random noise into coherent data samples. They operate by iteratively denoising a noisy input, guided by a neural network trained to predict the noise added at each step. This approach enables the synthesis of high-quality, diverse samples across various data modalities, particularly excelling in image and audio generation.

## History

**2015** — Sohl-Dickstein et al. introduced "Deep Unsupervised Learning using Nonequilibrium Thermodynamics," proposing Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (DDPMs) as a generative modeling framework inspired by statistical physics.

**2020** — Ho et al. significantly advanced DDPMs, demonstrating their ability to generate high-quality images competitive with GANs and VAEs, along with a simplified training objective.

**2020** — Song et al. concurrently developed "Score-Based Generative Modeling through Stochastic Differential Equations," establishing a theoretical connection between diffusion models and score-based models.

**2021** — Nichol et al. introduced improved DDPMs, enhancing sample quality and computational efficiency through techniques like cosine noise schedules and learned variance.

**2021** — Song et al. proposed Denoising Diffusion Implicit Models (DDIMs), enabling faster sampling and deterministic generation paths, which significantly reduced inference time.

**2022** — Rombach et al. introduced [Latent Diffusion](/algorithms/generative/diffusion/latent-diffusion) Models (LDMs), which perform the diffusion process in a compressed latent space, drastically reducing computational cost and memory requirements for high-resolution image generation.

## Mathematical Intuition

### Forward Diffusion Process

The forward process gradually adds Gaussian noise to an input data sample $x_0$ over $T$ discrete time steps, creating a sequence of noisy samples $x_1, x_2, ..., x_T$. Each step $t$ adds a small amount of noise, transforming $x_{t-1}$ into $x_t$ according to a fixed Markov chain. The variance schedule $eta_1, ..., eta_T$ controls the amount of noise added at each step. This allows for a direct sampling of $x_t$ from $x_0$ using the reparameterization trick.

$$
q(x_t | x_{t-1}) = \mathcal{N}(x_t; \sqrt{1 - \beta_t} x_{t-1}, \beta_t I)
$$

$$
q(x_t | x_0) = \mathcal{N}(x_t; \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t} x_0, (1 - \bar{\alpha}_t) I)
$$

$$
\alpha_t = 1 - \beta_t
$$

$$
\bar{\alpha}_t = \prod_{s=1}^t \alpha_s
$$

### Reverse Diffusion Process

The reverse process aims to invert the forward process, starting from pure Gaussian noise $x_T \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$ and iteratively denoising it to recover a data sample $x_0$. This reverse transition $q(x_{t-1} | x_t)$ is intractable, so a neural network $p_\theta(x_{t-1} | x_t)$ is trained to approximate it. The network typically predicts the noise $\epsilon_\theta(x_t, t)$ that was added at step $t$, which allows for an estimation of $x_0$ from $x_t$.

$$
q(x_{t-1} | x_t, x_0) = \mathcal{N}(x_{t-1}; \tilde{\mu}_t(x_t, x_0), \tilde{\beta}_t I)
$$

$$
\tilde{\mu}_t(x_t, x_0) = \frac{\sqrt{\alpha_t}(1 - \bar{\alpha}_{t-1})}{1 - \bar{\alpha}_t} x_t + \frac{\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}\beta_t}{1 - \bar{\alpha}_t} x_0
$$

$$
\tilde{\beta}_t = \frac{1 - \bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}{1 - \bar{\alpha}_t} \beta_t
$$

$$
\epsilon_\theta(x_t, t)
$$

### Training Objective Function

The training objective is to minimize the difference between the predicted noise $\epsilon_\theta(x_t, t)$ and the actual noise $\epsilon$ added at step $t$. This is a simplified version of the Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO) and is typically a mean squared error loss. The noisy sample $x_t$ is directly sampled from $x_0$ using the reparameterization trick, making the training efficient.

$$
\mathcal{L}_{simple} = \mathbb{E}_{t \sim U(1, T), x_0 \sim q(x_0), \epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)} \left[ ||\epsilon - \epsilon_\theta(\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t} x_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar{\alpha}_t} \epsilon, t)||^2 \right]
$$

$$
x_t = \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t} x_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar{\alpha}_t} \epsilon
$$

### Sampling New Data

To generate a new sample, one starts with a pure noise sample $x_T \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$. The trained neural network then iteratively denoises this sample by predicting the noise $\epsilon_\theta(x_t, t)$ at each step $t$ from $T$ down to $1$. This prediction is used to estimate $x_{t-1}$ from $x_t$, gradually refining the sample until a clean data sample $x_0$ is obtained.

$$
x_{t-1} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}} \left( x_t - \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1 - \bar{\alpha}_t}} \epsilon_\theta(x_t, t) \right) + \sigma_t z
$$

$$
z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)
$$

## Algorithm Steps

1. Define a forward diffusion process that gradually adds Gaussian noise to data samples $x_0$ over $T$ steps, resulting in noisy samples $x_t$.
2. Choose a neural network architecture, typically a U-Net, to serve as the noise predictor $\epsilon_\theta(x_t, t)$.
3. During training, for each data sample $x_0$ in a batch, randomly sample a time step $t \in [1, T]$.
4. Sample noise $\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$ and compute the noisy sample $x_t = \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t} x_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar{\alpha}_t} \epsilon$.
5. Pass $x_t$ and $t$ to the neural network to predict the noise $\epsilon_\theta(x_t, t)$.
6. Compute the loss as the mean squared error between the true noise $\epsilon$ and the predicted noise $\epsilon_\theta(x_t, t)$, then update the network parameters using gradient descent.
7. Repeat steps 3-6 until the network is sufficiently trained to accurately predict noise at various time steps.
8. For sampling, initialize $x_T \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$ and iteratively apply the reverse diffusion step from $t=T$ down to $t=1$ using the trained $\epsilon_\theta(x_t, t)$ to generate $x_{t-1}$, eventually yielding a clean sample $x_0$.

## Parameters

| Parameter | Type | Default | Range / Options | Description |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `num_diffusion_steps` | `integer` | `1000` | 1 to 2000 | The total number of discrete time steps $T$ in the forward and reverse diffusion process. |
| `beta_schedule` | `string` | `linear` | linear, cosine, quadratic, sigmoid | The schedule for the variance $\beta_t$ of the noise added at each step. Common options include "linear", "cosine", "quadratic". |
| `model_architecture` | `string` | `unet` | unet, transformer | The neural network architecture used as the noise predictor $\epsilon_\theta$. U-Net is the most common choice. |
| `optimizer` | `string` | `AdamW` | Adam, AdamW, SGD | The optimization algorithm used to train the neural network. |
| `learning_rate` | `float` | `1e-4` | 1e-6 to 1e-3 | The step size for the optimizer during training. |
| `loss_type` | `string` | `l2` | l1, l2, huber | The type of loss function used to compare predicted noise with true noise. "l2" (MSE) is standard. |

## Strengths

- Diffusion models are capable of generating highly realistic and diverse samples, often surpassing the quality of GANs in certain domains.
- Their training process is generally more stable compared to Generative Adversarial Networks, avoiding common issues like mode collapse.
- The architecture allows for flexible conditioning, enabling control over generated content through text prompts, class labels, or other inputs.
- They provide excellent mode coverage, meaning they can represent the full diversity of the training data distribution.
- The iterative denoising process offers a natural way to control the trade-off between sample quality and diversity during generation.
- The underlying mathematical framework is robust and well-understood, drawing connections to score-based models and stochastic differential equations.

## Weaknesses

- Sampling new data from diffusion models is computationally intensive and significantly slower than GANs or VAEs, requiring many sequential steps.
- Training diffusion models, especially for high-resolution data, demands substantial computational resources and memory.
- The large number of diffusion steps can lead to high inference latency, making real-time applications challenging.
- The choice of noise schedule and network architecture can significantly impact performance, requiring careful hyperparameter tuning.
- While stable, training can still be sensitive to learning rates and other optimization parameters.

## Use Cases

### Image Synthesis

Generating photorealistic images from text descriptions, creating novel artistic styles, or performing image-to-image translation.

### Audio Generation

Synthesizing realistic speech, music, or sound effects, often used in text-to-speech or music composition tools.

### Video Generation

Creating short video clips from text prompts or generating future frames in a sequence.

### Drug Discovery

Designing novel molecular structures with desired properties by treating molecules as graph-like data and diffusing in latent space.

### Medical Imaging

Generating synthetic medical images for data augmentation, privacy-preserving data sharing, or anomaly detection.

### Data Augmentation

Creating diverse synthetic data samples to expand training datasets for other machine learning tasks, especially in computer vision.
