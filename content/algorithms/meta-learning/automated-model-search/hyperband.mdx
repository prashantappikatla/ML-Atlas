## Overview

Hyperband is an efficient hyperparameter optimization algorithm that adaptively allocates computational resources to a large number of hyperparameter configurations. It achieves this by combining [Random Search](/algorithms/meta-learning/automated-model-search/random-search) with successive halving, iteratively evaluating configurations with increasing resources and pruning poorly performing ones. This strategy allows it to quickly identify promising configurations while minimizing wasted computation on unpromising candidates.

## History

**2016** — L. Li, K. Jamieson, G. DeSalvo, A. Rostamizadeh, and A. Talwalkar introduced Hyperband in their paper "Hyperband: A Novel Bandit-Based Approach to Hyperparameter Optimization," proposing an efficient method for hyperparameter tuning by leveraging successive halving.

**2017** — K. Jamieson and A. Talwalkar further explored the theoretical properties and practical implications of successive halving in "Non-stochastic Best Arm Identification and Hyperparameter Optimization," providing a deeper understanding of Hyperband's core component.

**2018** — S. Falkner, A. Klein, and F. Hutter combined Hyperband with [Bayesian Optimization](/algorithms/meta-learning/automated-model-search/bayesian-optimization) to create BOHB ([Bayesian Optimization](/algorithms/meta-learning/automated-model-search/bayesian-optimization) HyperBand), aiming to combine the best aspects of both approaches for improved performance and sample efficiency.

## Mathematical Intuition

### Successive Halving Principle

Successive Halving (SH) is a core component of Hyperband. It operates by uniformly sampling a set of hyperparameter configurations and evaluating them with a small amount of resources (e.g., epochs for a neural network). After this initial evaluation, only the top $1/\eta$ configurations (based on performance) are promoted to the next stage, where they are evaluated with $\eta$ times more resources. This process of evaluating, ranking, and pruning continues until only one configuration remains or a maximum resource budget is reached. The goal is to quickly discard poor configurations and focus resources on more promising ones.
$$ N_0 = N, \quad R_0 = r_{min} $$
For $k=0, 1, \dots, \log_\eta(R_{max}/r_{min})$:
$$ N_{k+1} = N_k / \eta, \quad R_{k+1} = R_k \cdot \eta $$
where $N$ is the initial number of configurations, $r_{min}$ is the minimum resource, $R_{max}$ is the maximum resource, and $\eta$ is the halving factor.

$$
N_0 = N, \quad R_0 = r_{min}
$$

$$
N_{k+1} = N_k / \eta, \quad R_{k+1} = R_k \cdot \eta
$$

### Hyperband Outer Loop Strategy

Hyperband addresses a limitation of Successive Halving: SH requires a fixed initial number of configurations $N$ and maximum resources $R_{max}$, which can be suboptimal if the best configuration is found with few resources or if the optimal $N$ is unknown. Hyperband solves this by running multiple independent Successive Halving "brackets." Each bracket $s$ starts with a different initial number of configurations $n_s$ and a corresponding maximum resource budget $r_s$, such that the total budget for each bracket is approximately constant. This ensures that Hyperband explores a wide range of $(N, R_{max})$ trade-offs.
The total budget $B$ is divided among $s_{max}+1$ brackets. For each bracket $s \in \{0, \dots, s_{max}\}$:
$$ n_s = \lfloor (B/R_{max}) \cdot \frac{\eta^{s_{max}-s}}{s_{max}-s+1} \rfloor $$
$$ r_s = R_{max} \cdot \eta^{-s} $$
where $R_{max}$ is the maximum resource allocated to any single configuration, and $s_{max} = \lfloor \log_\eta(R_{max}) \rfloor$. The initial resource for a configuration in bracket $s$ is $r_{initial,s} = r_s / \eta^{s_{max}-s}$.

$$
n_s = \lfloor (B/R_{max}) \cdot \frac{\eta^{s_{max}-s}}{s_{max}-s+1} \rfloor
$$

$$
r_s = R_{max} \cdot \eta^{-s}
$$

### Resource Allocation and Budget

The efficiency of Hyperband stems from its adaptive resource allocation. Instead of spending equal resources on all configurations, it strategically prunes less promising ones early. The total budget $B$ (e.g., total epochs, total training time) is a critical parameter. The halving factor $\eta$ determines how aggressively configurations are pruned and how quickly resources are increased. A larger $\eta$ prunes more aggressively but might discard good configurations prematurely if they need more resources to show their potential. The number of brackets $s_{max}+1$ ensures a diverse exploration of initial configurations and resource allocations.
The total budget for a single bracket $s$ is approximately:
$$ B_s \approx n_s \cdot r_{initial,s} \cdot \sum_{i=0}^{s_{max}-s} \eta^i = n_s \cdot r_{initial,s} \cdot \frac{\eta^{s_{max}-s+1}-1}{\eta-1} $$
This ensures that Hyperband explores various trade-offs between the number of configurations and the maximum resources allocated to each.

$$
B_s \approx n_s \cdot r_{initial,s} \cdot \sum_{i=0}^{s_{max}-s} \eta^i = n_s \cdot r_{initial,s} \cdot \frac{\eta^{s_{max}-s+1}-1}{\eta-1}
$$

## Algorithm Steps

1. Define the maximum resource $R_{max}$ that can be allocated to a single configuration and the halving factor $\eta$.
2. Determine the number of successive halving brackets, $s_{max} = \lfloor \log_\eta(R_{max}) \rfloor$.
3. For each bracket $s$ from $s_{max}$ down to $0$: calculate the number of configurations $n_s$ to sample and the initial resource $r_{initial,s}$ for each configuration, ensuring an approximately equal total budget across brackets.
4. Randomly sample $n_s$ unique hyperparameter configurations from the search space.
5. Execute a Successive Halving routine for these $n_s$ configurations:
6. For $i$ from $0$ to $s_{max}-s$: evaluate the current set of configurations using $r_{initial,s} \cdot \eta^i$ resources.
7. Keep the top $1/\eta$ performing configurations and discard the rest; if only one configuration remains or the maximum resource $R_{max}$ is reached for a configuration, exit the inner loop.
8. The hyperparameter configuration that yielded the best performance across all brackets and resource levels is selected as the optimal one.

## Strengths

- Hyperband is significantly more efficient than grid search or random search, especially when the evaluation of a configuration is computationally expensive.
- It effectively prunes unpromising hyperparameter configurations early, saving computational resources and time.
- The algorithm is highly parallelizable, as different brackets and configurations within a bracket can be evaluated independently.
- It does not require assumptions about the smoothness or structure of the objective function, making it robust across various machine learning tasks.
- Hyperband is less sensitive to the choice of initial hyperparameter values compared to Bayesian optimization methods.

## Weaknesses

- Hyperband requires a predefined maximum resource budget $R_{max}$ and a halving factor $\eta$, which can be challenging to set optimally without prior knowledge.
- It does not learn from past evaluations to guide future sampling, unlike Bayesian optimization, which can be less efficient in very low-dimensional search spaces.
- The algorithm might prematurely discard configurations that perform poorly with low resources but could excel with more resources.
- Its performance can be suboptimal if the best configurations are those that require very few resources to converge.

## Use Cases

### Deep Learning

Optimizing the learning rate, batch size, number of layers, and regularization parameters for neural networks in various applications.

### Computer Vision

Tuning hyperparameters for Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) used in image classification, object detection, and segmentation tasks.

### Natural Language Processing

Finding optimal configurations for Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs), Transformers, or other language models for tasks like text classification or machine translation.

### Reinforcement Learning

Optimizing policy network architectures and training parameters for agents learning in complex environments.

### General Machine Learning

Efficiently tuning hyperparameters for traditional models like Gradient Boosting Machines (e.g., [XGBoost](/algorithms/supervised/regression/xgboost), [LightGBM](/algorithms/supervised/regression/lightgbm)) or Support Vector Machines.
