## Overview

Quantile Regression models the relationship between predictors and specific conditional quantiles of the response variable, rather than just the conditional mean. This approach provides a more comprehensive understanding of the effect of covariates across the entire distribution of the dependent variable, making it robust to outliers and heteroscedasticity. Unlike Ordinary Least Squares, it allows for varying effects of predictors at different parts of the response distribution, offering insights into distributional changes. It is particularly useful when the interest lies beyond the average effect, focusing on lower or upper tails of the distribution.

## History

**1978** — Roger Koenker and Gilbert Bassett introduce Quantile Regression in their seminal paper 'Regression Quantiles,' extending the concept of least squares to conditional quantiles.

**1987** — Koenker and Portnoy develop efficient algorithms for computing quantile regression estimates, leveraging linear programming techniques, which significantly advanced its practical applicability.

**1994** — Koenker and Machado propose a method for goodness-of-fit in quantile regression, analogous to $R^2$ in OLS, providing a measure for model evaluation.

**2005** — Roger Koenker publishes 'Quantile Regression,' a comprehensive book that consolidates the theoretical foundations, computational methods, and diverse applications of the method, establishing it as a distinct field.

## Mathematical Intuition

### Conditional Quantiles Defined

Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) estimates the conditional mean of the response variable given the predictors. In contrast, Quantile Regression estimates the conditional $\tau$-th quantile, where $\tau \in (0, 1)$. The $\tau$-th conditional quantile $Q_Y(\tau | X=x)$ is the value $y$ such that the probability of the response $Y$ being less than or equal to $y$, given predictors $X=x$, is $\tau$. For example, $\tau=0.5$ corresponds to the conditional median, while $\tau=0.25$ and $\tau=0.75$ correspond to the first and third conditional quartiles, respectively. This allows for a more granular analysis of how covariates affect different parts of the response distribution.

$$
Q_Y(\tau | X=x)
$$

$$
P(Y \le y | X=x) = \tau
$$

### Pinball Loss Function

The core of Quantile Regression lies in minimizing an asymmetric loss function, often called the 'pinball loss' or 'check function,' denoted $\rho_\tau(u)$. For a given quantile $\tau$, the loss function is defined as: $$ \rho_\tau(u) = u(\tau - I(u < 0)) $$ where $u$ is the residual ($y_i - x_i^T \beta_\tau$) and $I(\cdot)$ is the indicator function. This function penalizes under-prediction and over-prediction asymmetrically. Specifically, for positive residuals ($u > 0$), the penalty is $\tau u$, and for negative residuals ($u < 0$), the penalty is $(1-\tau)|u|$. The objective function for a linear quantile regression model $y_i = x_i^T \beta_\tau + \epsilon_i$ is to find the coefficient vector $\beta_\tau$ that minimizes the sum of these losses over all observations: $$ \min_{\beta_\tau} \sum_{i=1}^n \rho_\tau(y_i - x_i^T \beta_\tau) $$ When $\tau=0.5$, the pinball loss simplifies to the absolute error, making median regression equivalent to Least Absolute Deviations (LAD) regression.

$$
\rho_\tau(u) = u(\tau - I(u < 0))
$$

$$
\min_{\beta_\tau} \sum_{i=1}^n \rho_\tau(y_i - x_i^T \beta_\tau)
$$

### Optimization via Linear Programming

The objective function for Quantile Regression, $\sum_{i=1}^n \rho_\tau(y_i - x_i^T \beta_\tau)$, is convex but not differentiable everywhere due to the absolute value component in the pinball loss. However, this minimization problem can be reformulated as a linear programming (LP) problem. By introducing non-negative slack variables $e_i^+$ and $e_i^-$ such that $y_i - x_i^T \beta_\tau = e_i^+ - e_i^-$, the problem can be written as: $$ \min_{\beta_\tau, e^+, e^-} \left( \sum_{i=1}^n \tau e_i^+ + (1-\tau) e_i^- \right) $$ subject to the constraints: $$ y_i - x_i^T \beta_\tau = e_i^+ - e_i^- \quad \forall i=1, \dots, n $$ $$ e_i^+, e_i^- \ge 0 \quad \forall i=1, \dots, n $$ This transformation allows standard linear programming solvers (e.g., simplex method, interior-point methods) to efficiently find the optimal $\beta_\tau$ coefficients. The computational complexity is generally higher than OLS but well-managed by modern LP algorithms.

$$
\sum_{i=1}^n \rho_\tau(y_i - x_i^T \beta_\tau)
$$

$$
y_i - x_i^T \beta_\tau = e_i^+ - e_i^-
$$

$$
\min_{\beta_\tau, e^+, e^-} \left( \sum_{i=1}^n \tau e_i^+ + (1-\tau) e_i^- \right)
$$

$$
y_i - x_i^T \beta_\tau = e_i^+ - e_i^- \quad \forall i=1, \dots, n
$$

$$
e_i^+, e_i^- \ge 0 \quad \forall i=1, \dots, n
$$

## Algorithm Steps

1. Select a specific quantile $\tau \in (0, 1)$ for which the regression coefficients are to be estimated.
2. Define the linear model for the $\tau$-th conditional quantile as $Q_Y(\tau | X=x) = x^T \beta_\tau$, where $x$ is the vector of predictor variables and $\beta_\tau$ is the vector of quantile-specific coefficients.
3. Formulate the optimization problem to minimize the sum of the 'pinball loss' (or check function) over the residuals $y_i - x_i^T \beta_\tau$: $\min_{\beta_\tau} \sum_{i=1}^n \rho_\tau(y_i - x_i^T \beta_\tau)$.
4. Transform this non-differentiable minimization problem into an equivalent linear programming problem by introducing non-negative slack variables $e_i^+$ and $e_i^-$.
5. Solve the resulting linear programming problem using an appropriate optimization algorithm (e.g., simplex method or interior-point method) to obtain the estimated coefficient vector $\hat{\beta}_\tau$.
6. The obtained $\hat{\beta}_\tau$ represents the estimated effect of the predictors on the $\tau$-th conditional quantile of the response variable.

## Parameters

| Parameter | Type | Default | Range / Options | Description |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `quantile` | `float` | `0.5` | (0, 1) | The quantile to estimate, which must be a value between 0 and 1. A value of 0.5 corresponds to median regression. |

| `max_iter` | `int` | `1000` | > 0 | Maximum number of iterations for the solver to converge. |
| `tol` | `float` | `1e-6` | > 0 | Tolerance for termination of the optimization algorithm. The solver stops when the duality gap is below this value. |

## Strengths

- Provides a more complete picture of the relationship between variables by modeling different parts of the conditional distribution, not just the mean.
- Robust to outliers in the response variable because it minimizes absolute errors (for the median) or asymmetric absolute errors, rather than squared errors.
- Naturally handles heteroscedasticity, as the effects of predictors can vary across quantiles, allowing for different slopes at different parts of the distribution.
- Does not assume a specific distribution for the error term, only that the $\tau$-th conditional quantile of the error is zero.
- Useful for detecting and quantifying heterogeneous effects of covariates on the response, which might be masked by mean regression.
- The estimates are equivariant to monotone transformations of the response variable, meaning the quantile of a transformed variable is the transformed quantile of the original variable.

## Weaknesses

- Computationally more intensive than Ordinary Least Squares, especially for large datasets, as it typically involves solving a linear programming problem.
- Interpretation of coefficients can be less intuitive than OLS coefficients, as they refer to specific quantiles rather than the conditional mean.
- Requires careful selection of the quantile $\tau$, and analyzing multiple quantiles can increase the complexity of model interpretation and presentation.
- Standard statistical inference (e.g., p-values, confidence intervals) can be more complex to derive and compute than for OLS, often requiring bootstrapping or specialized methods.
- Can be sensitive to the choice of regularization parameter (alpha) when applied in a regularized form.

## Use Cases

### Economics

Analyzing wage gaps across different income quantiles to understand factors affecting low-income vs. high-income earners, revealing how education or experience impacts different parts of the wage distribution.

### Environmental Science

Modeling the impact of pollutants on the upper quantiles of health outcomes, such as asthma attacks, to identify risk factors for the most vulnerable populations.

### Finance

Predicting value-at-risk (VaR) by modeling the lower quantiles of portfolio returns to assess downside risk, providing a more robust measure than mean-based forecasts.

### Medicine

Investigating how treatments affect different parts of a patient's recovery distribution, not just the average recovery time, to understand effects on slow vs. fast responders.

### Education

Studying factors influencing student performance at different achievement levels (e.g., low-performing vs. high-performing students) to tailor interventions more effectively.

### Sports Analytics

Analyzing factors that influence athlete performance at different levels, such as the 90th percentile of speed or endurance, to identify traits of elite performers.
