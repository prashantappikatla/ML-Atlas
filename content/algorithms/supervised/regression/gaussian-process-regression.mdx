## Overview

[Gaussian Process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaussian_process) Regression (GPR) is a non-parametric Bayesian approach to regression that models the distribution over functions directly, rather than learning a fixed function. It defines a prior distribution over possible functions and updates it to a posterior distribution given observed data. This method provides not only point predictions but also a measure of uncertainty associated with those predictions, making it valuable for tasks requiring confidence intervals and risk assessment.

## History

Williams and [Carl Edward Rasmussen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaussian_process) published "[Gaussian Processes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaussian_process) for Regression," a seminal paper that formalized the modern approach to GPR and its application in machine learning.

**2006** — Carl Edward Rasmussen and Christopher K. I. **2010** — Research intensified on developing sparse and approximate Gaussian Process methods to overcome the cubic computational complexity for large datasets, enabling broader applicability.

## Mathematical Intuition

### Gaussian Process Definition

A Gaussian Process (GP) is a collection of random variables, any finite number of which have a joint Gaussian distribution. It is fully specified by its mean function $m(\mathbf{x})$ and covariance function (kernel) $k(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}')$. Formally, a function $f(\mathbf{x})$ is drawn from a GP if $f(\mathbf{x}) \sim \mathcal{GP}(m(\mathbf{x}), k(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}'))$. For simplicity, the mean function is often assumed to be zero, as any non-zero mean can be incorporated into the kernel.

$$
f(\mathbf{x}) \sim \mathcal{GP}(m(\mathbf{x}), k(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}'))
$$

### Role of Kernel Functions

The kernel function $k(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}')$ quantifies the similarity between two input points $\mathbf{x}$ and $\mathbf{x}'$ and determines the smoothness, periodicity, and other properties of the functions drawn from the GP. A common choice is the [Radial Basis Function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radial_basis_function) (RBF) kernel, also known as the squared exponential kernel, which is infinitely differentiable and produces very smooth functions. Its form is given by:

$$
k(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}') = \sigma_f^2 \exp\left(-\frac{\|\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}'\|^2}{2l^2}\right)
$$

### Predictive Distribution Derivation

Given training data $\mathcal{D} = \{(\mathbf{X}, \mathbf{y})\}$ and assuming noisy observations $y = f(\mathbf{x}) + \epsilon$ where $\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_n^2)$, the posterior distribution over functions at new test points $\mathbf{X}_*$ is also Gaussian. The predictive mean $\bar{f}(\mathbf{X}_*)$ and covariance $\text{cov}(f(\mathbf{X}_*))$ are derived from the joint Gaussian distribution of training and test outputs:

$$
\bar{f}(\mathbf{X}_*) = K(\mathbf{X}_*, \mathbf{X}) [K(\mathbf{X}, \mathbf{X}) + \sigma_n^2 I]^{-1} \mathbf{y}
$$

$$
\text{cov}(f(\mathbf{X}_*)) = K(\mathbf{X}_*, \mathbf{X}_*) - K(\mathbf{X}_*, \mathbf{X}) [K(\mathbf{X}, \mathbf{X}) + \sigma_n^2 I]^{-1} K(\mathbf{X}, \mathbf{X}_*)
$$

### Hyperparameter Optimization via Marginal Likelihood

The kernel function typically contains hyperparameters (e.g., length scale $l$, signal variance $\sigma_f^2$, noise variance $\sigma_n^2$). These hyperparameters are optimized by maximizing the marginal likelihood (or evidence) of the training data, which integrates out the function $f$. This objective function balances data fit with model complexity, preventing overfitting. The log marginal likelihood is given by:

$$
\log p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{X}, \theta) = -\frac{1}{2} \mathbf{y}^T (K + \sigma_n^2 I)^{-1} \mathbf{y} - \frac{1}{2} \log |K + \sigma_n^2 I| - \frac{N}{2} \log(2\pi)
$$

## Algorithm Steps

1. Define a suitable kernel function $k(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}')$ that encodes prior beliefs about the function's properties, such as smoothness or periodicity.
2. Initialize the kernel's hyperparameters, which control its specific behavior (e.g., length scale, amplitude, noise variance).
3. Compute the kernel matrix $K(\mathbf{X}, \mathbf{X})$ for the training data $\mathbf{X}$, where $K_{ij} = k(\mathbf{x}_i, \mathbf{x}_j)$.
4. Add a noise term $\sigma_n^2 I$ to the kernel matrix to account for observation noise, forming $K_y = K(\mathbf{X}, \mathbf{X}) + \sigma_n^2 I$.
5. Optimize the kernel hyperparameters by maximizing the marginal likelihood $p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{X}, \theta)$ with respect to $\theta$, often using gradient-based optimization.
6. For new test points $\mathbf{X}_*$, compute the kernel matrices $K(\mathbf{X}_*, \mathbf{X})$ and $K(\mathbf{X}_*, \mathbf{X}_*)$ using the optimized hyperparameters.
7. Calculate the predictive mean $\bar{f}(\mathbf{X}_*)$ and predictive covariance $\text{cov}(f(\mathbf{X}_*))$ using the derived formulas.
8. Return the predictive mean as the point estimate and the diagonal of the predictive covariance as the uncertainty measure for each prediction.

## Strengths

- Gaussian Process Regression provides well-calibrated uncertainty estimates alongside point predictions, which is crucial for decision-making under uncertainty.
- Its non-parametric nature allows it to model complex, non-linear relationships without assuming a specific functional form.
- Prior knowledge about the function's properties can be effectively incorporated through the careful choice and design of the kernel function.
- GPR is effective with small datasets, as its Bayesian framework naturally handles limited data without severe overfitting.
- The marginal likelihood provides a principled and robust method for optimizing hyperparameters and performing model selection.

## Weaknesses

- The computational complexity of GPR is typically $O(N^3)$ for training and $O(N^2)$ for prediction, where $N$ is the number of training samples, making it challenging for large datasets.
- The memory complexity is $O(N^2)$ for storing the kernel matrix, which limits its applicability to datasets with many samples.
- The choice and design of the kernel function significantly impact performance and often require domain knowledge or extensive experimentation.
- GPR can struggle with high-dimensional input spaces due to the 'curse of dimensionality' affecting kernel performance and requiring more data to cover the space.
- The optimization of hyperparameters can be prone to local optima, especially with complex kernels or many restarts.

## Use Cases

### Robotics

Used for path planning, control, and sensor fusion, where quantifying uncertainty in predictions is critical for safe and robust operation.

### Environmental Modeling

Applied to predict air quality, water levels, or climate variables, providing forecasts with associated confidence intervals for risk assessment.

### Financial Forecasting

Employed for modeling stock prices, economic indicators, or market trends, offering uncertainty bounds for investment decisions and risk management.

### Hyperparameter Optimization

Serves as a surrogate model in [Bayesian Optimization](/algorithms/meta-learning/automated-model-search/bayesian-optimization), efficiently exploring the hyperparameter space of other machine learning models by predicting performance and uncertainty.

### Geostatistics

Known as Kriging, it is used for spatial interpolation of geological or environmental data, estimating values at unobserved locations with uncertainty.

### Drug Discovery

Predicting molecular properties or reaction outcomes, guiding experimental design and accelerating the discovery of new compounds.
