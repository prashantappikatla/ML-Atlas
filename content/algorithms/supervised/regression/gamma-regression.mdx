## Overview

Gamma Regression is a type of [Generalized Linear Model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generalized_linear_model) (GLM) specifically designed to model positive, continuous, and often right-skewed response variables. It assumes that the response variable follows a Gamma distribution and uses a link function to relate the expected value of the response to a linear combination of the predictor variables. This approach is particularly useful when the variance of the response variable is proportional to the square of its mean, a common characteristic of many real-world positive-valued datasets.

## Mathematical Intuition

### Generalized Linear Models Framework

A GLM consists of three core components: a random component specifying the probability distribution of the response variable (e.g., Gamma), a systematic component (linear predictor) $ \eta = X\beta $, and a link function $ g(\mu) = \eta $ that connects the expected value of the response $ \mu = E[Y] $ to the linear predictor. This framework allows for modeling a wide range of response types beyond normal distributions.

$$
\eta = X\beta
$$

$$
g(\mu) = \eta
$$

$$
\mu = E[Y]
$$

### Gamma Distribution Properties

The Gamma distribution is a two-parameter continuous probability distribution defined for positive real numbers, making it suitable for strictly positive response variables. Its probability density function (PDF) is given by:$$ f(y; \alpha, \beta) = \frac{\beta^\alpha}{\Gamma(\alpha)} y^{\alpha-1} e^{-\beta y} \quad \text{for } y > 0 $$where $ \alpha $ is the shape parameter and $ \beta $ is the rate parameter. In the context of GLMs, it is often re-parameterized by its mean $ \mu = \frac{\alpha}{\beta} $ and variance $ Var(Y) = \frac{\alpha}{\beta^2} = \frac{\mu^2}{\alpha} $. This re-parameterization highlights that the variance is proportional to the square of the mean, a key characteristic that Gamma Regression can model.

$$
f(y; \alpha, \beta) = \frac{\beta^\alpha}{\Gamma(\alpha)} y^{\alpha-1} e^{-\beta y}
$$

$$
y > 0
$$

$$
\mu = \frac{\alpha}{\beta}
$$

$$
Var(Y) = \frac{\alpha}{\beta^2} = \frac{\mu^2}{\alpha}
$$

### Link Functions for Gamma

The link function $ g(\mu) $ transforms the expected value of the response $ \mu $ to the linear predictor $ \eta $. For the Gamma distribution, the canonical link function is the inverse link, $ g(\mu) = 1/\mu $. However, the log link, $ g(\mu) = \log(\mu) $, is more commonly used in practice. The log link ensures that the predicted mean $ \mu = e^\eta $ is always positive, which is consistent with the domain of the Gamma distribution, and often provides more interpretable coefficients.

$$
g(\mu) = 1/\mu
$$

$$
g(\mu) = \log(\mu)
$$

$$
\mu = e^\eta
$$

### Parameter Estimation via MLE

The model parameters $ \beta $ (regression coefficients) are typically estimated using [Maximum Likelihood Estimation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximum_likelihood_estimation) (MLE). This involves finding the values of $ \beta $ that maximize the likelihood function, which is the product of the Gamma probability density functions for all observed responses given the predictors. Since the likelihood equations are generally non-linear, an iterative optimization algorithm, such as [Iteratively Reweighted Least Squares](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iteratively_reweighted_least_squares) (IRLS), is commonly employed to find the optimal coefficients.

## Algorithm Steps

1. Specify the Gamma distribution as the random component for the response variable $ Y $, assuming $ Y > 0 $.
2. Define the systematic component (linear predictor) as $ \eta = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \dots + \beta_p x_p $.
3. Choose a link function $ g(\mu) $ (e.g., log link $ g(\mu) = \log(\mu) $ or inverse link $ g(\mu) = 1/\mu $) to connect the expected value $ \mu = E[Y] $ to the linear predictor $ \eta $.
4. Formulate the likelihood function based on the Gamma probability density function for all observations.
5. Use an iterative optimization algorithm, such as Iteratively Reweighted Least Squares (IRLS), to find the regression coefficients $ \beta $ that maximize the likelihood function.
6. Iterate until the change in coefficients or likelihood falls below a specified tolerance or a maximum number of iterations is reached.
7. The final estimated coefficients $ \hat{\beta} $ define the Gamma Regression model, allowing for predictions of the mean response.

## Strengths

- It effectively models positive, continuous response variables that exhibit right-skewness and heteroscedasticity.
- The model naturally handles situations where the variance of the response increases with its mean, specifically when variance is proportional to the squared mean.
- Coefficients can be interpreted in terms of multiplicative effects on the mean response when using a log link function.
- It provides a statistically sound framework for data where traditional Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) assumptions are violated.
- The model's predictions are guaranteed to be positive, which is appropriate for many real-world quantities.
- It is robust to the exact shape of the Gamma distribution, as long as the mean-variance relationship holds.

## Weaknesses

- The assumption that the response variable follows a Gamma distribution might not hold for all datasets, leading to poor model fit.
- It can be sensitive to outliers, especially those with very small values, which can disproportionately influence parameter estimates.
- Convergence issues can arise during the iterative parameter estimation process, particularly with poorly scaled data or highly correlated predictors.
- Interpreting coefficients with non-canonical link functions can be less straightforward than with a simple linear model.
- The model requires the response variable to be strictly positive; zero values must be handled separately or transformed.

## Use Cases

### Insurance

Modeling the total cost of claims or the amount of individual claims, which are typically positive, continuous, and right-skewed.

### Finance

Predicting credit loss amounts, duration of financial transactions, or asset returns, where values are positive and often have a heavy tail.

### Environmental Science

Analyzing pollutant concentrations, rainfall amounts, or species abundance, which are non-negative and often exhibit skewed distributions.

### Healthcare

Estimating hospital stay durations, medical expenditure, or drug dosage effects, where values are positive and can vary widely.

### Economics

Modeling income distribution, sales revenue, or economic growth rates, which are positive and often right-skewed.

### Reliability Engineering

Predicting the lifetime of components or systems, which are positive continuous variables often following a Gamma-like distribution.
