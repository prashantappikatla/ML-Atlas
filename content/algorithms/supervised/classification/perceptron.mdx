## Overview

The [Perceptron](/algorithms/supervised/perceptron) is a foundational supervised learning algorithm for binary classification, inspired by the human brain's neuron. It constructs a linear decision boundary to separate data points belonging to different classes. Distinctively, it employs a simple error-driven learning rule to iteratively adjust its weights until all training examples are correctly classified, provided the data is linearly separable.

## History

**1957** — [Frank Rosenblatt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frank_Rosenblatt) introduces the Perceptron algorithm at Cornell Aeronautical Laboratory, marking a significant step in neural network research.

**1958** — Rosenblatt publishes "The Perceptron: A Probabilistic Model for Information Storage and Organization in the Brain," detailing its architecture and learning rule.

**1960** — Rosenblatt proves the Perceptron Convergence Theorem, demonstrating that the algorithm will converge to a solution if the data is linearly separable.

**1969** — Marvin Minsky and Seymour Papert publish "Perceptrons," highlighting its limitations, particularly its inability to solve non-linearly separable problems like XOR, which contributed to the "AI winter."

**1980** — The development of multi-layer perceptrons and the backpropagation algorithm overcomes the limitations of the single-layer perceptron, leading to a resurgence in neural network research.

## Mathematical Intuition

### Linear Model and Decision Boundary

The Perceptron models a binary classification problem by attempting to find a hyperplane that separates the two classes. For an input feature vector $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$, the model computes a weighted sum of its features, $z = w^T x + b$, where $w \in \mathbb{R}^n$ is the weight vector and $b \in \mathbb{R}$ is the bias term. The decision boundary is defined by the equation $w^T x + b = 0$. The predicted class $\hat{y}$ is determined by the sign of $z$.
$$ z = w^T x + b $$
$$ \hat{y} = \text{sign}(z) = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } z \ge 0 \\ -1 & \text{if } z < 0 \end{cases} $$

$$

$$

### Perceptron Learning Rule

The Perceptron learns by iteratively updating its weights and bias based on misclassified training examples. If a training example $(x_i, y_i)$ is misclassified, the weights are adjusted to move the decision boundary closer to correctly classifying that point. The update rule for weights $w$ and bias $b$ for a misclassified example is:
$$ w \leftarrow w + \eta y_i x_i $$
$$ b \leftarrow b + \eta y_i $$
Here, $\eta$ is the learning rate, a small positive constant that controls the step size of the updates. The update moves the weight vector in the direction of the misclassified point if it was predicted as negative, or away from it if predicted as positive, effectively correcting the classification.

$$
w \leftarrow w + \eta y_i x_i
$$

$$
b \leftarrow b + \eta y_i
$$

### Perceptron Convergence Theorem

The Perceptron Convergence Theorem states that if the training data is linearly separable, the Perceptron algorithm is guaranteed to converge in a finite number of steps. This means it will find a weight vector $w$ and bias $b$ that correctly classify all training examples. The number of updates depends on the margin of separation and the norm of the input vectors. However, if the data is not linearly separable, the algorithm will never converge and will oscillate indefinitely.

## Algorithm Steps

1. Initialize the weight vector $w$ and bias $b$ to small random values or zeros.
2. Set a learning rate $\eta$ and a maximum number of epochs (iterations over the entire dataset).
3. For each epoch, iterate through each training example $(x_i, y_i)$ in the dataset.
4. Compute the predicted output $\hat{y}_i$ for the current example using the activation function: $\hat{y}_i = \text{sign}(w^T x_i + b)$.
5. If the prediction is incorrect (i.e., $\hat{y}_i \ne y_i$), update the weights and bias using the Perceptron learning rule: $w \leftarrow w + \eta y_i x_i$ and $b \leftarrow b + \eta y_i$.
6. Repeat steps 3-5 until all examples are correctly classified or the maximum number of epochs is reached.

## Strengths

- The Perceptron is simple to understand and implement, making it a good starting point for learning about neural networks.
- It has a theoretical guarantee of convergence for linearly separable datasets, finding a separating hyperplane in a finite number of steps.
- The algorithm is computationally efficient, requiring minimal resources for training and prediction, especially for large datasets.
- Its linear decision boundary can be highly interpretable, as the weights directly indicate the importance of each feature in the classification.
- It can serve as a robust baseline model for simple binary classification tasks where linear separability is expected.

## Weaknesses

- The Perceptron can only classify linearly separable data; it fails to converge if the classes cannot be perfectly separated by a hyperplane.
- It is sensitive to outliers, as a single misclassified point can significantly alter the decision boundary during the weight update process.
- The algorithm does not output probabilities, only a hard classification, which limits its use in applications requiring confidence scores.
- Its performance can be poor on noisy datasets, as it continuously tries to correct misclassifications, potentially leading to overfitting.
- The final decision boundary found by the Perceptron is not unique and can vary depending on the initial weights and the order of training examples.

## Use Cases

### Logic Gates

Used to model basic logic functions like AND, OR, and NOT, which are linearly separable.

### Spam Detection

Can be applied for simple binary classification of emails as spam or not spam based on a bag-of-words representation.

### Medical Diagnosis

Employed as a preliminary tool for classifying certain medical conditions that exhibit clear linear patterns in patient data.

### Image Recognition (Simple)

Historically used for basic pattern recognition tasks, such as distinguishing between two simple shapes or characters after feature extraction.

### Credit Scoring

Can serve as a simple model to classify loan applicants as high or low risk based on financial attributes.
