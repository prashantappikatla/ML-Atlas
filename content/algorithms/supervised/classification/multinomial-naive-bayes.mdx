## Overview

Multinomial [Naive Bayes](/algorithms/supervised/classification/naive-bayes) is a probabilistic classification algorithm particularly well-suited for discrete features, such as word counts in text classification. It operates on Bayes' theorem with a strong "naive" assumption of conditional independence between features given the class. This simplicity allows for efficient training and prediction, making it a popular choice for high-dimensional datasets like those found in natural language processing. Its generative approach models the distribution of features for each class.

## History

**1950** — The foundational concept of [Naive Bayes](/algorithms/supervised/classification/naive-bayes), based on Bayes' theorem and the assumption of feature independence, began to emerge in early pattern recognition and statistical classification literature.

**1960** — Early applications of probabilistic classifiers, including those implicitly using [Naive Bayes](/algorithms/supervised/classification/naive-bayes) principles, appeared in fields like medical diagnosis and document retrieval.

**1990** — With the rise of the internet and large text corpora, [Naive Bayes](/algorithms/supervised/classification/naive-bayes), particularly its multinomial variant, gained significant prominence in text classification tasks such as spam filtering and document categorization.

**1998** — Andrew McCallum and Kamal Nigam published "A Comparison of Event Models for [Naive Bayes](/algorithms/supervised/classification/naive-bayes) Text Classification," which rigorously compared different event models, including the multinomial model, solidifying its theoretical and practical understanding in NLP.

**2000** — Multinomial [Naive Bayes](/algorithms/supervised/classification/naive-bayes) was widely adopted and implemented in various machine learning libraries and tools due to its simplicity, efficiency, and competitive performance on many text-related problems.

## Mathematical Intuition

### Bayes' Theorem Foundation

The core of [Naive Bayes](/algorithms/supervised/classification/naive-bayes) is Bayes' Theorem, which allows us to calculate the posterior probability of a class $C_k$ given a feature vector $x = (x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n)$.
$$P(C_k | x) = \frac{P(x | C_k) P(C_k)}{P(x)}$$
For classification, we are interested in finding the class $C_k$ that maximizes this posterior probability. Since $P(x)$ is constant for all classes, we can simplify the decision rule to:
$$C_{predict} = \arg\max_{C_k} P(x | C_k) P(C_k)$$

$$
P(C_k | x) = \frac{P(x | C_k) P(C_k)}{P(x)}
$$

$$
C_{predict} = \arg\max_{C_k} P(x | C_k) P(C_k)
$$

### The Naive Independence Assumption

The "naive" part comes from assuming that all features $x_i$ are conditionally independent of each other given the class $C_k$. This simplifies the likelihood $P(x | C_k)$ significantly:
$$P(x | C_k) = P(x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n | C_k) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} P(x_i | C_k)$$
Substituting this into the classification rule yields the [Naive Bayes](/algorithms/supervised/classification/naive-bayes) classifier:
$$C_{predict} = \arg\max_{C_k} P(C_k) \prod_{i=1}^{n} P(x_i | C_k)$$
To avoid numerical underflow with many small probabilities, the product is often computed in the log-domain:
$$C_{predict} = \arg\max_{C_k} \left[ \log P(C_k) + \sum_{i=1}^{n} \log P(x_i | C_k) \right]$$

$$
P(x | C_k) = P(x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n | C_k) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} P(x_i | C_k)
$$

$$
C_{predict} = \arg\max_{C_k} P(C_k) \prod_{i=1}^{n} P(x_i | C_k)
$$

$$
C_{predict} = \arg\max_{C_k} \left[ \log P(C_k) + \sum_{i=1}^{n} \log P(x_i | C_k) \right]
$$

### Multinomial Feature Model

For Multinomial [Naive Bayes](/algorithms/supervised/classification/naive-bayes), features $x_i$ typically represent counts (e.g., word occurrences in a document). The likelihood $P(x_i | C_k)$ is modeled using a multinomial distribution. Specifically, for a document $d$ and a class $C_k$, the probability of observing a word $w_j$ is estimated as:
$$P(w_j | C_k) = \frac{\text{count}(w_j, C_k) + \alpha}{\sum_{w \in V} (\text{count}(w, C_k) + \alpha)}$$
where $\text{count}(w_j, C_k)$ is the number of times word $w_j$ appears in all documents of class $C_k$, $V$ is the vocabulary, and $\alpha$ is the Laplace smoothing parameter (typically $\alpha=1$). The likelihood of a document $x$ (represented as a bag of words) given a class $C_k$ is then:
$$P(x | C_k) = P(\text{document } d | C_k) = \frac{(\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i)!}{\prod_{i=1}^{n} x_i!} \prod_{i=1}^{n} P(w_i | C_k)^{x_i}$$
However, in practice, the multinomial coefficient $\frac{(\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i)!}{\prod_{i=1}^{n} x_i!}$ is often ignored as it is constant for all classes, and the classification rule simplifies to using only the product of individual word probabilities raised to their counts:
$$P(x | C_k) \propto \prod_{i=1}^{n} P(w_i | C_k)^{x_i}$$

$$
P(w_j | C_k) = \frac{\text{count}(w_j, C_k) + \alpha}{\sum_{w \in V} (\text{count}(w, C_k) + \alpha)}
$$

$$
P(x | C_k) = P(\text{document } d | C_k) = \frac{(\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i)!}{\prod_{i=1}^{n} x_i!} \prod_{i=1}^{n} P(w_i | C_k)^{x_i}
$$

$$
P(x | C_k) \propto \prod_{i=1}^{n} P(w_i | C_k)^{x_i}
$$

### Parameter Estimation with Smoothing

The prior probability of a class $C_k$ is estimated from the training data as the proportion of documents belonging to that class:
$$P(C_k) = \frac{\text{Number of documents in class } C_k}{\text{Total number of documents}}$$
The conditional probabilities $P(w_j | C_k)$ are estimated using maximum likelihood estimation with Laplace smoothing to prevent zero probabilities for unseen words. The smoothing parameter $\alpha$ (often called `alpha` in implementations) adds $\alpha$ to each word count and $\alpha \times |V|$ to the total count for a given class, where $|V|$ is the size of the vocabulary. This ensures that even words not seen in a particular class during training still have a non-zero probability.

$$
P(C_k) = \frac{\text{Number of documents in class } C_k}{\text{Total number of documents}}
$$

## Algorithm Steps

1. Prepare the training data by converting documents into feature vectors, typically using a bag-of-words representation where each feature $x_i$ is the count of a specific word in the document.
2. For each class $C_k$, calculate the prior probability $P(C_k)$ by dividing the number of training documents in class $C_k$ by the total number of training documents.
3. For each word $w_j$ in the vocabulary and each class $C_k$, calculate the conditional probability $P(w_j | C_k)$ using Laplace smoothing: $P(w_j | C_k) = \frac{\text{count}(w_j, C_k) + \alpha}{\sum_{w \in V} (\text{count}(w, C_k) + \alpha)}$.
4. For a new, unseen document $x$ to be classified, extract its feature vector (word counts).
5. For each class $C_k$, compute the log-posterior probability (or a value proportional to it) using the Naive Bayes formula: $\log P(C_k) + \sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i \log P(w_i | C_k)$.
6. Assign the document $x$ to the class $C_k$ that yields the highest computed log-posterior probability.

## Parameters

## Strengths

- It is computationally efficient and scales well to large datasets and high-dimensional feature spaces.
- Multinomial Naive Bayes often performs surprisingly well in text classification tasks, even with its strong independence assumption.
- It is simple to implement and interpret, making it a good baseline model for many classification problems.
- The model requires a relatively small amount of training data to estimate the necessary parameters.
- It handles multiple classes naturally without requiring one-vs-rest or one-vs-one strategies.
- The algorithm is robust to irrelevant features, as their probabilities tend to be uniformly distributed across classes.

## Weaknesses

- The core "naive" assumption of conditional independence between features given the class is rarely true in real-world data, which can lead to suboptimal probability estimates.
- While it often performs well in classification, the probability outputs from Multinomial Naive Bayes are not always reliable or well-calibrated.
- It cannot capture interactions between features, as each feature contributes independently to the final decision.
- The model can be sensitive to the distribution of features, performing best when features are truly counts or frequencies.

## Use Cases

### Spam Detection

Classifying emails as spam or legitimate based on the frequency of words in their content.

### Sentiment Analysis

Determining the emotional tone (positive, negative, neutral) of text reviews, social media posts, or customer feedback.

### Document Categorization

Automatically assigning documents to predefined categories, such as news articles into sports, politics, or technology.

### Author Identification

Identifying the author of a text based on their unique writing style and word usage patterns.

### Language Identification

Determining the language of a given text snippet by analyzing word frequencies.
