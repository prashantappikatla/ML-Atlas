## Overview

RotatE is a knowledge graph embedding model that represents entities and relations as vectors in a complex vector space. It models relations as element-wise rotations from head entities to tail entities, enabling it to capture various relation patterns such as symmetry, anti-symmetry, inversion, and composition. This rotational transformation provides a highly expressive mechanism for learning complex relational structures within knowledge graphs, leading to state-of-the-art performance on link prediction tasks.

## History

**2013** — [Word2Vec](/algorithms/deep-learning/self-supervised-learning/word2vec) by Mikolov et al. introduced skip-gram and CBOW models for learning word embeddings, inspiring subsequent work on entity and relation embeddings in knowledge graphs.

**2014** — [TransE](/algorithms/graph/knowledge-graphs/transe) by Bordes et al. proposed a foundational translational model for knowledge graph embeddings, representing relations as translations in a real vector space.

**2016** — ComplEx by Trouillon et al. extended knowledge graph embedding models to complex vector spaces, allowing for better modeling of symmetric and anti-symmetric relations by representing entities and relations as complex vectors.

**2019** — RotatE was introduced by Sun et al., pioneering the concept of modeling relations as element-wise rotations in a complex vector space, significantly enhancing the ability to capture diverse relational patterns.

**2020** — HAKE by Zhang et al. built upon RotatE by introducing a hierarchical [Attention Mechanism](/algorithms/deep-learning/sequence-models/attention-mechanism) to better distinguish entities at different levels of abstraction within knowledge graphs.

## Mathematical Intuition

### Knowledge Graph Triples

A knowledge graph consists of triples $(h, r, t)$, where $h$ is the head entity, $r$ is the relation, and $t$ is the tail entity. RotatE embeds each entity $e \in \mathcal{E}$ and relation $r \in \mathcal{R}$ into a complex vector space $\mathbb{C}^k$, where $k$ is the embedding dimension. Each component of these vectors is a complex number.

$$
h, t \in \mathbb{C}^k
$$

$$
r \in \mathbb{C}^k
$$

### Rotational Transformation Principle

RotatE models a relation $r$ as an element-wise rotation from the head entity $h$ to the tail entity $t$. For each dimension $i$, the relation component $r_i$ is represented as a complex number with a modulus of 1, i.e., $|r_i|=1$. This constraint ensures that the transformation is a pure rotation in the complex plane for each dimension, preserving the magnitude of the entity embeddings.

$$
h_i \circ r_i \approx t_i
$$

$$
|r_i|=1
$$

### Plausibility Score Function

The score function for a triple $(h, r, t)$ measures its plausibility. It is defined as the negative distance between the rotated head entity and the tail entity. The distance is typically the $L_1$ or $L_2$ norm of the difference in the complex vector space, where $h \circ r$ denotes the element-wise product $(h_1 r_1, \dots, h_k r_k)$. A lower (more negative) score indicates higher plausibility.

$$
f(h, r, t) = -\|h \circ r - t\|_{L_1}
$$

$$
f(h, r, t) = -\|h \circ r - t\|_{L_2}
$$

### Margin-Based Loss Function

RotatE is typically trained using a margin-based ranking loss function with negative sampling. For each positive triple $(h, r, t)$, a set of negative triples $(h', r, t')$ or $(h, r, t'')$ is generated by corrupting either the head or tail entity. The loss function aims to ensure that positive triples have a higher score (less negative) than negative triples by a certain margin $\gamma$. The relation embeddings $r$ are continuously projected onto the unit circle such that $|r_i|=1$ for all dimensions $i$.

$$
\mathcal{L} = \sum_{(h,r,t) \in S} \sum_{(h',r',t') \in S'} \max(0, \gamma + f(h,r,t) - f(h',r',t'))
$$

## Algorithm Steps

1. Initialize entity embeddings $h, t \in \mathbb{C}^k$ and relation embeddings $r \in \mathbb{C}^k$ randomly, typically from a uniform distribution.
2. Normalize the modulus of each component of the relation embeddings to 1, ensuring that $|r_i|=1$ for all $i$ and thus representing pure rotations.
3. For each training epoch, iterate through mini-batches of positive triples $(h, r, t)$ from the knowledge graph.
4. For each positive triple, generate a set of negative triples by corrupting either the head entity (e.g., $(h', r, t)$) or the tail entity (e.g., $(h, r, t')$) with randomly chosen entities.
5. Compute the score $f(h, r, t) = -\|h \circ r - t\|_{L_1}$ for positive triples and $f(h', r', t')$ for negative triples.
6. Calculate the margin-based ranking loss: $\mathcal{L} = \sum \max(0, \gamma + f(h,r,t) - f(h',r',t'))$, where $\gamma$ is the margin hyperparameter.
7. Update entity and relation embeddings using an optimizer (e.g., Adam) to minimize the loss function through backpropagation.
8. After each update, re-normalize the modulus of each component of the relation embeddings to 1 to maintain the rotational property.

## Parameters

## Strengths

- RotatE can effectively model various complex relational patterns, including symmetry, anti-symmetry, inversion, and composition, due to its rotational transformation in complex space.
- It achieves state-of-the-art performance on link prediction and knowledge graph completion benchmarks across several datasets.
- The use of complex vector space provides a rich representation capacity for entities and relations, allowing for more nuanced modeling of relationships.
- The rotational property ensures that the magnitude of embeddings is preserved during transformations, which can lead to more stable and robust learning.
- It offers a unified framework for capturing diverse relation types, reducing the need for specialized models for each type of relationship.

## Weaknesses

- Training RotatE can be computationally intensive, especially for very large knowledge graphs, due to the complex number operations and extensive negative sampling.
- The model's performance is sensitive to hyperparameter tuning, such as embedding dimension, margin, and learning rate, requiring careful optimization.
- Interpreting the learned complex embeddings and rotational transformations can be challenging compared to simpler real-vector models.
- Scalability to extremely dense or sparse knowledge graphs might require advanced sampling or optimization techniques beyond the basic formulation.
- The constraint on relation embeddings to have a modulus of 1 can sometimes limit expressiveness for certain types of relations that might benefit from magnitude changes.

## Use Cases

### Knowledge Graph Completion

Predicting missing entities or relations in an incomplete knowledge graph to enrich its content and improve its utility.

### Link Prediction

Identifying plausible new links between existing entities in a knowledge graph, useful for discovering new facts or relationships.

### Question Answering

Enhancing the accuracy of knowledge graph-based question answering systems by leveraging the structured knowledge and inferred relationships to find answers.

### Recommender Systems

Improving recommendation quality by modeling user-item interactions and item-item relationships as a knowledge graph, then predicting missing links.

### Drug Discovery

Discovering new drug-target interactions or disease-gene associations by completing biomedical knowledge graphs, accelerating research.

### Fraud Detection

Identifying suspicious patterns or missing links in financial transaction graphs that might indicate fraudulent activities, enhancing security.
