## Overview

LINE (Large-scale Information Network Embedding) is a graph embedding algorithm designed to preserve both first-order and second-order proximities in large-scale information networks. It learns low-dimensional vector representations for nodes by optimizing two separate objective functions, one for each type of proximity. This dual-objective approach allows it to capture both direct neighbor relationships and shared neighborhood structures effectively, making it suitable for various downstream tasks.

## History

**2015** — Jian Tang, Meng Qu, Mingzhe Wang, Ming Zhang, Jun Yan, and Qiaozhu Mei introduced LINE, addressing the challenge of embedding large-scale graphs while preserving different orders of proximity.

**2016** — Subsequent research began exploring extensions and optimizations of LINE, adapting its proximity preservation principles for specific graph types and applications.

**2017** — LINE's explicit modeling of first and second-order proximity significantly influenced the development of many subsequent graph embedding methods, particularly those focused on preserving structural information.

## Mathematical Intuition

### First-order Proximity Preservation

First-order proximity between two nodes $u$ and $v$ refers to the direct link between them. If $u$ and $v$ are directly connected, they are considered to have high first-order proximity. LINE models this by defining the probability of an edge $(u,v)$ based on the dot product of their embeddings $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$ using a sigmoid function. The objective function $O_1$ aims to minimize the Kullback-Leibler divergence between the empirical edge distribution and the model's predicted distribution.

$$
$$p_1(v|u) = \frac{1}{1 + \exp(-\mathbf{u}^T \mathbf{v})}$$
$$

$$
$$O_1 = -\sum_{(u,v) \in E} w_{uv} \log p_1(v|u)$$
$$

$$
where $w_{uv}$ is the weight of the edge $(u,v)$.
$$

### Second-order Proximity Preservation

Second-order proximity between nodes $u$ and $v$ refers to the similarity of their neighborhood structures. If $u$ and $v$ share many common neighbors, they have high second-order proximity. LINE models this by predicting a node's context (its neighbors) from its embedding. For this, each node $v$ has two embeddings: $\mathbf{v}$ (as a node) and $\mathbf{v'}$ (as a context). The objective function $O_2$ aims to predict the context of $u$ using its embedding $\mathbf{u}$ and the context embeddings $\mathbf{v'}$ of its neighbors.

$$
$$p_2(v|u) = \frac{\exp(\mathbf{u}^T \mathbf{v'})}{\sum_{k \in V} \exp(\mathbf{u}^T \mathbf{k'})}$$
$$

$$
$$O_2 = -\sum_{(u,v) \in E} w_{uv} \log p_2(v|u)$$
$$

### Optimization with Negative Sampling

Directly computing the normalization term in $p_2(v|u)$ (the sum over all nodes $k \in V$) is computationally expensive for large graphs. To address this, LINE employs negative sampling, a technique borrowed from word embedding models like [Word2Vec](/algorithms/deep-learning/self-supervised-learning/word2vec). For each observed edge $(u,v)$, the objective is to maximize the probability of $v$ being a neighbor of $u$ while minimizing the probability of $K$ randomly sampled 'negative' nodes $v_i$ being neighbors. This significantly speeds up training.

$$
$$J_{neg} = \log \sigma(\mathbf{u}^T \mathbf{v}) + \sum_{i=1}^K E_{v_i \sim P_n(v)} [\log \sigma(-\mathbf{u}^T \mathbf{v_i})]$$
$$

$$
where $\sigma(x) = 1/(1+\exp(-x))$ is the sigmoid function, and $P_n(v)$ is the negative sampling distribution, typically proportional to node degree.
$$

### Combining Learned Embeddings

LINE trains two separate models: one for first-order proximity and another for second-order proximity. Each model learns an embedding vector for every node. After training, the final embedding for a node $u$ is obtained by concatenating its first-order embedding $\mathbf{u}$ and its second-order embedding $\mathbf{u'}$. This combined representation aims to capture a richer set of structural information from the graph.

## Algorithm Steps

1. Initialize two sets of node embeddings: $\mathbf{u}$ for first-order proximity and $\mathbf{u'}$ for second-order proximity, both randomly with a specified dimension.
2. Optimize the first-order proximity objective $O_1$ using stochastic gradient descent, where $O_1$ aims to maximize the probability of observed edges $(u,v)$ based on $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$. Negative sampling is used to efficiently approximate the normalization term.
3. Optimize the second-order proximity objective $O_2$ using stochastic gradient descent, where $O_2$ aims to maximize the probability of observed edges $(u,v)$ based on $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v'}$. Negative sampling is also applied here.
4. After training both models for a specified number of epochs, concatenate the learned first-order embedding $\mathbf{u}$ and second-order embedding $\mathbf{u'}$ for each node $u$ to form its final representation.
5. The resulting concatenated embeddings serve as the low-dimensional feature vectors for nodes, ready for downstream machine learning tasks.

## Parameters

| Parameter | Type | Default | Range / Options | Description |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `embedding_dim` | `int` | `128` | positive integer | The dimension of the learned node embeddings for each order of proximity. The final embedding dimension will be twice this value if both orders are used. |
| `order` | `int` | `3` | {1, 2, 3} | Specifies which orders of proximity to preserve: 1 for first-order only, 2 for second-order only, or 3 for both (concatenated). |
| `learning_rate` | `float` | `0.025` | positive float | The initial learning rate for the stochastic gradient descent optimizer. |
| `n_negative_samples` | `int` | `5` | positive integer | The number of negative samples to use for each positive edge during training with negative sampling. |
| `epochs` | `int` | `10` | positive integer | The number of training epochs (iterations over the entire dataset) for each proximity model. |
| `batch_size` | `int` | `1024` | positive integer | The number of edges processed in each batch during stochastic gradient descent. |
| `seed` | `int` | `None` | integer or None | Seed for the random number generator to ensure reproducibility of results. |

## Strengths

- Effectively preserves both direct neighbor relationships (first-order proximity) and shared neighborhood structures (second-order proximity).
- Scalable to large-scale graphs due to its efficient use of negative sampling and stochastic gradient descent for optimization.
- Can handle weighted and unweighted graphs, making it versatile for various network types.
- The two proximity models can be trained independently, allowing for parallelization and efficient computation.
- Produces dense, low-dimensional vector representations suitable for various downstream machine learning tasks.
- The explicit modeling of different orders of proximity provides a clear theoretical foundation.

## Weaknesses

- Requires careful tuning of hyperparameters such as embedding dimension, learning rate, and number of negative samples, which can be dataset-dependent.
- Does not inherently capture higher-order proximities beyond second-order, which might be crucial in some complex networks.
- The concatenation of two independently trained embeddings might not always yield the globally optimal combined representation.
- Performance can degrade on extremely sparse graphs where many nodes have very few connections, making proximity estimation less reliable.
- The model is transductive, meaning it cannot directly generate embeddings for new, unseen nodes without retraining or specific extension mechanisms.

## Use Cases

### Social Networks

Identifying influential users, recommending friends, or detecting communities based on learned user similarity in social graphs.

### Recommender Systems

Embedding user-item interaction graphs to generate item recommendations for users or suggest users for specific items.

### Knowledge Graphs

Learning representations of entities and relations for tasks like link prediction, entity classification, or knowledge graph completion.

### Bioinformatics

Analyzing protein-protein interaction networks or gene regulatory networks to discover functional relationships and disease mechanisms.

### Cybersecurity

Detecting anomalous behavior, identifying attack patterns, or classifying malicious entities in network traffic graphs.

### Natural Language Processing

Embedding word co-occurrence networks to capture semantic relationships between words, similar to word embedding models.
