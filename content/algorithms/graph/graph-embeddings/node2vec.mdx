## Overview

Node2Vec generates low-dimensional feature representations (embeddings) for nodes in a graph, preserving neighborhood structure. It achieves this by employing a biased random walk procedure that flexibly explores neighborhoods, interpolating between Breadth-First Search (BFS) and Depth-First Search (DFS) strategies. These generated walks are then fed into a Skip-gram model, similar to [Word2Vec](/algorithms/deep-learning/self-supervised-learning/word2vec), to learn the node embeddings. The algorithm is distinctive for its ability to capture diverse notions of node similarity through its tunable random walk parameters.

## History

**2013** — [Word2Vec](/algorithms/deep-learning/self-supervised-learning/word2vec) was introduced by Mikolov et al., establishing the Skip-gram model for learning word embeddings, which Node2Vec later adapts for nodes.

**2014** — [DeepWalk](/algorithms/graph/graph-embeddings/deepwalk), by Perozzi et al., adapted the Skip-gram model to learn node embeddings from uniformly random walks on graphs, pioneering the field of graph embeddings.

**2016** — Node2Vec was introduced by Grover and Leskovec in their seminal paper, proposing a novel biased random walk strategy to capture richer structural information in graphs.

**2017** — [GraphSAGE](/algorithms/graph/graph-embeddings/graphsage) by Hamilton et al. generalized Node2Vec by learning an aggregation function for node features, enabling inductive learning on unseen nodes.

**2018** — Further advancements in graph embeddings, such as LINE and SDNE, offered alternative approaches focusing on different orders of node proximity.

## Mathematical Intuition

### Objective Function for Embeddings

Node2Vec aims to learn a mapping $f: V \to \mathbb{R}^d$ that maps each node $u \in V$ to a $d$-dimensional vector representation. The objective function maximizes the likelihood of observing a node's neighborhood given its embedding, similar to [Word2Vec](/algorithms/deep-learning/self-supervised-learning/word2vec):
$$ \max_{f} \sum_{u \in V} \log P(N_S(u) | f(u)) $$
Here, $N_S(u)$ represents the neighborhood of node $u$ generated by a specific sampling strategy $S$. The core innovation lies in the flexible and biased random walk strategy $S$.

$$
f: V \to \mathbb{R}^d
$$

$$
\max_{f} \sum_{u \in V} \log P(N_S(u) | f(u))
$$

### Biased Random Walk Strategy

The sampling strategy $S$ in Node2Vec interpolates between Breadth-First Search (BFS) and Depth-First Search (DFS) behaviors. BFS-like walks tend to explore immediate neighbors, capturing structural equivalence (nodes with similar local roles), while DFS-like walks explore further away, capturing homophily (nodes belonging to similar communities). This interpolation is controlled by two hyperparameters: $p$ (return parameter) and $q$ (in-out parameter).

### Transition Probabilities Calculation

Given a current node $v$ and a previous node $t$, the probability of transitioning to a neighbor $x$ is defined as:
$$ P(x | v, t) = \frac{\alpha_{pq}(t, x) \cdot w_{vx}}{Z} $$
where $w_{vx}$ is the edge weight between $v$ and $x$, and $Z$ is a normalization constant. The bias term $\alpha_{pq}(t, x)$ is crucial and depends on the shortest path distance $d_{tx}$ between $t$ and $x$:
$$ \alpha_{pq}(t, x) = \begin{cases} 1/p & \text{if } d_{tx} = 0 \\ 1 & \text{if } d_{tx} = 1 \\ 1/q & \text{if } d_{tx} = 2 \end{cases} $$
Here, $d_{tx}=0$ means $x$ is the same as $t$, $d_{tx}=1$ means $x$ is a direct neighbor of $t$, and $d_{tx}=2$ means $x$ is not $t$ and not a direct neighbor of $t$. Parameter $p$ controls the likelihood of immediately re-visiting the previous node, while $q$ controls the likelihood of exploring nodes further away.

$$
P(x | v, t) = \frac{\alpha_{pq}(t, x) \cdot w_{vx}}{Z}
$$

$$
\alpha_{pq}(t, x) = \begin{cases} 1/p & \text{if } d_{tx} = 0 \\ 1 & \text{if } d_{tx} = 1 \\ 1/q & \text{if } d_{tx} = 2 \end{cases}
$$

### Skip-gram Model for Learning

After generating a corpus of random walks, these sequences of nodes are treated as 'sentences' where nodes are 'words'. The Skip-gram model, originally from [Word2Vec](/algorithms/deep-learning/self-supervised-learning/word2vec), is then employed to learn the node embeddings. For each node $u$ in a walk, Skip-gram predicts its context nodes $N_S(u)$ within a fixed window size. The conditional probability $P(N_S(u) | f(u))$ is typically modeled using softmax:
$$ P(n_i | f(u)) = \frac{\exp(f(n_i) \cdot f(u))}{\sum_{v \in V} \exp(f(v) \cdot f(u))} $$
This objective is optimized using [Stochastic Gradient Descent](/algorithms/meta-learning/optimization/stochastic-gradient-descent), often with negative sampling for computational efficiency, to obtain the final $d$-dimensional embedding for each node.

$$
P(n_i | f(u)) = \frac{\exp(f(n_i) \cdot f(u))}{\sum_{v \in V} \exp(f(v) \cdot f(u))}
$$

## Algorithm Steps

1. Given a graph $G=(V, E, W)$ with nodes $V$, edges $E$, and optional edge weights $W$, initialize node embeddings randomly.
2. Precompute the normalized transition probabilities for all possible (previous node, current node, next node) triplets based on the $p$ and $q$ parameters.
3. For each node $u \in V$, generate a fixed number of random walks (e.g., `num_walks`) of a specified length (e.g., `walk_length`).
4. Each random walk starts at a random node and proceeds by selecting the next node based on the precomputed biased transition probabilities.
5. Collect all generated random walks, treating each walk as a sequence of nodes, forming a corpus.
6. Feed these sequences of nodes into a Skip-gram model (or CBOW) to learn node embeddings.
7. Optimize the Skip-gram objective using stochastic gradient descent, typically with negative sampling, to obtain the final $d$-dimensional embedding for each node.

## Parameters

| Parameter | Type | Default | Range / Options | Description |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `dimensions` | `int` | `128` | positive integer | Dimensionality of the node embeddings. |
| `walk_length` | `int` | `80` | positive integer | Length of each random walk generated for every node. |
| `num_walks` | `int` | `10` | positive integer | Number of random walks to generate starting from each node. |
| `p` | `float` | `1.0` | positive float | Return hyperparameter, controlling the probability of immediately re-visiting the previous node in a walk. |
| `q` | `float` | `1.0` | positive float | In-out hyperparameter, controlling the probability of exploring nodes far away from the previous node (DFS-like behavior) versus closer nodes (BFS-like behavior). |
| `window_size` | `int` | `10` | positive integer | Maximum distance between the current node and predicted context nodes within a random walk (Skip-gram parameter). |
| `epochs` | `int` | `5` | positive integer | Number of iterations (epochs) over the entire corpus of random walks during Skip-gram training. |
| `workers` | `int` | `1` | positive integer | Number of worker threads to use for training the Skip-gram model. |
| `min_count` | `int` | `1` | non-negative integer | Ignores all nodes with total frequency lower than this during Skip-gram training. |
| `negative_samples` | `int` | `5` | non-negative integer | Number of negative samples to use for each positive sample during Skip-gram training, improving efficiency. |

## Strengths

- It provides a flexible framework for exploring graph neighborhoods by interpolating between BFS and DFS strategies.
- The learned embeddings effectively capture both structural equivalence and homophily properties of nodes.
- Node2Vec is applicable to various types of graphs, including unweighted, weighted, directed, and undirected graphs.
- The algorithm is highly parallelizable, as random walks can be generated independently and the Skip-gram training can be distributed.
- It generates dense, low-dimensional vector representations that are suitable for various downstream machine learning tasks.
- The embeddings are robust to noise and missing information in the graph structure to some extent.

## Weaknesses

- Node2Vec is inherently transductive, meaning it cannot directly generate embeddings for new, unseen nodes without retraining or significant modifications.
- The computational cost of generating random walks and training the Skip-gram model can be high for very large graphs.
- Selecting optimal values for the hyperparameters $p$, $q$, walk length, and number of walks often requires extensive tuning.
- It does not directly incorporate node features or edge features, relying solely on the graph topology.
- The quality of embeddings can be sensitive to the connectivity and density of the input graph.

## Use Cases

### Recommendation Systems

Embedding user and item nodes in a bipartite graph to predict user preferences or suggest items based on learned similarities.

### Fraud Detection

Identifying anomalous nodes (e.g., fraudulent accounts, suspicious transactions) in financial or social networks by analyzing their learned embeddings.

### Drug Discovery

Representing molecules as graphs and embedding their nodes (atoms) to predict molecular properties, interactions, or potential drug candidates.

### Social Network Analysis

Clustering users with similar interests or identifying communities within social networks based on their learned node embeddings.

### Bioinformatics

Analyzing protein-protein interaction networks to predict protein functions, identify disease-related pathways, or understand biological processes.

### Knowledge Graphs

Learning embeddings for entities and relations within knowledge graphs to perform tasks like link prediction, entity classification, or question answering.
