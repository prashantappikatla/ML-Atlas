## Overview

[Probabilistic Latent Semantic Analysis](/algorithms/unsupervised/probabilistic-latent-semantic-analysis) (PLSA), also known as Probabilistic Latent Semantic Indexing (PLSI), is a statistical technique for the analysis of two-mode and co-occurrence data, particularly in natural language processing. It models each word in a document as being generated by a single topic, and each document as a mixture of topics. PLSA aims to discover the underlying latent semantic structure of a document collection by factorizing the document-term co-occurrence matrix into topic-specific word distributions and document-specific topic distributions.

## History

**1999** — Thomas Hofmann introduces Probabilistic [Latent Semantic Analysis](/algorithms/unsupervised/latent-semantic-analysis) (PLSA) as a statistical model for text and other discrete data, providing a probabilistic foundation for latent semantic modeling.

**2003** — David Blei, [Andrew Ng](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrew_Ng), and [Michael Jordan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_I._Jordan) introduce [Latent Dirichlet Allocation](/algorithms/unsupervised/latent-dirichlet-allocation) (LDA), a generative probabilistic model that addresses some of PLSA's limitations, such as its lack of a generative model for new documents and its tendency to overfit.

## Mathematical Intuition

### Generative Process for Documents

PLSA posits a generative process where each word $w$ in a document $d$ is generated by first choosing a topic $z$ according to a document-specific probability distribution $P(z|d)$, and then choosing a word $w$ from a topic-specific probability distribution $P(w|z)$. This implies that documents are mixtures of topics, and topics are distributions over words. The joint probability of observing a document $d$ and a word $w$ within that document is given by marginalizing over the latent topic $z$:$$ P(d, w) = P(d) P(w|d) = P(d) \sum_{z \in \mathcal{Z}} P(w|z) P(z|d) $$Here, $P(d)$ is the probability of observing a document $d$, $P(z|d)$ is the probability of a topic $z$ given a document $d$, and $P(w|z)$ is the probability of a word $w$ given a topic $z$. The parameters to be estimated are $P(w|z)$ and $P(z|d)$.

$$
P(d, w) = P(d) P(w|d) = P(d) \sum_{z \in \mathcal{Z}} P(w|z) P(z|d)
$$

### Parameter Estimation via EM

The parameters $P(w|z)$ and $P(z|d)$ are typically estimated using the Expectation-Maximization (EM) algorithm. The EM algorithm iteratively refines the parameter estimates by alternating between two steps: the E-step and the M-step. The E-step computes the posterior probabilities of the latent topics given the observed words and current parameter estimates. Specifically, for each word occurrence $(d,w)$, the posterior probability of topic $z$ is calculated as:$$ P(z|d,w) = \frac{P(w|z) P(z|d)}{\sum_{z'} P(w|z') P(z'|d)} $$This step essentially assigns each word occurrence to a topic probabilistically.

$$
P(z|d,w) = \frac{P(w|z) P(z|d)}{\sum_{z'} P(w|z') P(z'|d)}
$$

### Maximizing Expected Log-Likelihood

In the M-step, the parameters $P(w|z)$ and $P(z|d)$ are updated to maximize the expected log-likelihood of the data, where the expectation is taken with respect to the posterior probabilities computed in the E-step. The update rules are derived by setting the derivatives of the expected log-likelihood with respect to the parameters to zero, subject to the probability constraints (summing to one). The update equations are:$$ P(w|z) = \frac{\sum_d n(d,w) P(z|d,w)}{\sum_{w'} \sum_d n(d,w') P(z|d,w')} $$$$ P(z|d) = \frac{\sum_w n(d,w) P(z|d,w)}{\sum_w n(d,w)} $$Here, $n(d,w)$ denotes the frequency of word $w$ in document $d$. These updates ensure that the parameters are consistent with the topic assignments from the E-step.

$$
P(w|z) = \frac{\sum_d n(d,w) P(z|d,w)}{\sum_{w'} \sum_d n(d,w') P(z|d,w')}
$$

$$
P(z|d) = \frac{\sum_w n(d,w) P(z|d,w)}{\sum_w n(d,w)}
$$

## Algorithm Steps

1. Initialize the topic-word probabilities $P(w|z)$ and document-topic probabilities $P(z|d)$ randomly, ensuring they are normalized (e.g., $\sum_w P(w|z) = 1$ for all $z$, and $\sum_z P(z|d) = 1$ for all $d$).
2. Repeat until convergence (e.g., the change in log-likelihood falls below a specified tolerance or a maximum number of iterations is reached):
3. E-step (Expectation): For each word occurrence $n(d,w)$ in each document $d$, compute the posterior probability of each topic $z$ given the document and word using the current parameter estimates: $P(z|d,w) = \frac{P(w|z) P(z|d)}{\sum_{z'} P(w|z') P(z'|d)}$.
4. M-step (Maximization): Update the parameters $P(w|z)$ and $P(z|d)$ using the posterior probabilities computed in the E-step.
5. Update $P(w|z)$ for all words $w$ and topics $z$: $P(w|z) = \frac{\sum_d n(d,w) P(z|d,w)}{\sum_{w'} \sum_d n(d,w') P(z|d,w')}$.
6. Update $P(z|d)$ for all topics $z$ and documents $d$: $P(z|d) = \frac{\sum_w n(d,w) P(z|d,w)}{\sum_w n(d,w)}$.
7. Calculate the log-likelihood of the data with the updated parameters to monitor convergence.

## Parameters

| Parameter | Type | Default | Range / Options | Description |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `n_components` | `integer` | `10` | integer > 0 | The number of latent topics to discover in the document collection. |
| `max_iter` | `integer` | `100` | integer > 0 | The maximum number of Expectation-Maximization (EM) iterations to perform. |
| `tol` | `float` | `1e-4` | float > 0 | The tolerance for convergence, where the algorithm stops if the change in log-likelihood between iterations is below this value. |
| `random_state` | `integer or None` | `None` | integer ≥ 0 or None | Seed for the random number generator, used for reproducibility of initial parameter values. |
| `epsilon` | `float` | `1e-10` | float ≥ 0 | A small value added to probabilities to prevent division by zero or log of zero during calculations. |

## Strengths

- PLSA provides interpretable topics, as each topic is explicitly represented by a distribution over words.
- It effectively handles synonymy and polysemy by mapping words and documents to a lower-dimensional latent semantic space.
- The probabilistic framework offers a clear statistical interpretation of the latent topics and their relationships to documents and words.
- It can discover meaningful semantic structures in text corpora, which is valuable for information retrieval and document analysis.
- The model is relatively straightforward to understand conceptually compared to more complex generative models.
- PLSA can be applied to any co-occurrence data, not just text, making it versatile for various domains.

## Weaknesses

- PLSA is not a generative model for new documents, meaning it cannot assign probabilities to documents not seen during training.
- The number of parameters grows linearly with the number of documents, making it prone to overfitting, especially with small corpora.
- Its computational complexity can be high for very large document collections, as it scales linearly with the number of documents and words.
- The number of latent topics must be specified in advance, which can be challenging to determine optimally.
- The EM algorithm can converge to local optima, and the final solution may depend on the initial parameter randomization.

## Use Cases

### Information Retrieval

Improving search relevance by matching queries to documents based on latent topics rather than just keyword matching, enhancing the understanding of document content.

### Document Classification

Using the topic distributions of documents as features for classification tasks, allowing for categorization based on semantic content rather than just word presence.

### Recommendation Systems

Recommending documents or items to users based on their inferred topic preferences, by modeling user interests as distributions over topics.

### Bioinformatics

Analyzing gene expression data to discover latent biological pathways or disease subtypes, treating genes as 'words' and samples as 'documents'.

### Sentiment Analysis

Identifying underlying sentiment topics in customer reviews or social media posts, revealing common themes associated with positive or negative opinions.

### Image Annotation

Discovering latent concepts in image collections by treating image regions or features as 'words' and images as 'documents', aiding in automatic annotation.
