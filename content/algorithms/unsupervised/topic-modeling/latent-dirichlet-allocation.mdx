## Overview

[Latent Dirichlet Allocation](/algorithms/unsupervised/latent-dirichlet-allocation) (LDA) is a generative probabilistic model for collections of discrete data, such as text corpora. It models each document as a mixture of a small number of topics, and each topic as a mixture of words. LDA aims to discover the underlying "topics" that pervade a collection of documents, providing a low-dimensional representation of the text data. This unsupervised approach allows for the organization, summarization, and exploration of large document archives.

## History

**2003** — David Blei, [Andrew Ng](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrew_Ng), and [Michael Jordan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_I._Jordan) introduced Latent Dirichlet Allocation in their seminal paper "Latent Dirichlet Allocation," establishing a foundational probabilistic framework for topic modeling.

**2004** — Thomas Griffiths and Mark Steyvers published "Finding Scientific Topics," demonstrating LDA's utility in analyzing large collections of scientific abstracts and introducing practical inference methods.

**2006** — David Blei and John Lafferty introduced the Correlated Topic Model (CTM), an extension of LDA that allows for correlations between topics, addressing a limitation of LDA's independence assumption.

**2006** — David Blei and John Lafferty also introduced the Dynamic Topic Model (DTM), an extension of LDA for modeling the evolution of topics over time in sequential document collections.

**2007** — Phan, Nguyen, and Horiguchi proposed the use of Gibbs sampling for inference in LDA, offering an alternative to the original variational EM algorithm and often simplifying implementation.

**2012** — Matthew Hoffman, David Blei, and Francis Bach introduced Online Variational Bayes for LDA, enabling efficient inference on very large datasets by processing documents in mini-batches.

## Mathematical Intuition

### Generative Process of Documents

LDA posits a generative process for how documents in a corpus are created. For each document $d$ in the corpus, a distribution over topics $\theta_d$ is drawn from a Dirichlet prior $\text{Dir}(\alpha)$. Then, for each word position $w_{d,n}$ in document $d$, a topic $z_{d,n}$ is sampled from $\theta_d$. Finally, a word $w_{d,n}$ is sampled from the word distribution $\phi_{z_{d,n}}$ corresponding to the chosen topic $z_{d,n}$, where $\phi_k$ is drawn from another Dirichlet prior $\text{Dir}(\eta)$. This process implies that words within a document are generated by different topics, and topics are shared across documents.
$$
\begin{aligned}
\phi_k &\sim \text{Dir}(\eta) \quad \text{for } k=1, \dots, K \\
\theta_d &\sim \text{Dir}(\alpha) \quad \text{for } d=1, \dots, M \\
z_{d,n} &\sim \text{Multinomial}(\theta_d) \quad \text{for } n=1, \dots, N_d \\
w_{d,n} &\sim \text{Multinomial}(\phi_{z_{d,n}})
\end{aligned}
$$
where $K$ is the number of topics, $M$ is the number of documents, $N_d$ is the number of words in document $d$, $\alpha$ is the document-topic prior, and $\eta$ is the topic-word prior.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\phi_k &\sim \text{Dir}(\eta) \quad \text{for } k=1, \dots, K \\
\theta_d &\sim \text{Dir}(\alpha) \quad \text{for } d=1, \dots, M \\
z_{d,n} &\sim \text{Multinomial}(\theta_d) \quad \text{for } n=1, \dots, N_d \\
w_{d,n} &\sim \text{Multinomial}(\phi_{z_{d,n}})
\end{aligned}
$$

### Dirichlet Priors for Distributions

The Dirichlet distribution is a conjugate prior for the multinomial distribution, which is crucial for LDA's mathematical tractability. In LDA, the Dirichlet distribution is used to model the prior distributions over two key components: the topic distribution for each document ($\theta_d$) and the word distribution for each topic ($\phi_k$). The hyperparameters $\alpha$ and $\eta$ control the sparsity of these distributions. A small $\alpha$ encourages documents to have only a few topics, while a small $\eta$ encourages topics to have only a few words. These priors introduce a smoothing effect and allow for robust estimation even with limited data.
$$
\text{Dir}(\mathbf{x} | \boldsymbol{\alpha}) = \frac{1}{B(\boldsymbol{\alpha})} \prod_{i=1}^K x_i^{\alpha_i - 1}
$$
where $B(\boldsymbol{\alpha})$ is the multivariate Beta function, and $\sum x_i = 1$, $x_i \ge 0$.

$$
\text{Dir}(\mathbf{x} | \boldsymbol{\alpha}) = \frac{1}{B(\boldsymbol{\alpha})} \prod_{i=1}^K x_i^{\alpha_i - 1}
$$

### Inference of Latent Variables

The primary challenge in LDA is inferring the latent variables: the topic assignments for each word ($z_{d,n}$), the topic distributions for each document ($\theta_d$), and the word distributions for each topic ($\phi_k$). Directly computing the posterior distribution $P(\theta, \phi, z | w, \alpha, \eta)$ is intractable due to the coupling between $\theta$ and $\phi$. Therefore, approximate inference techniques are employed. Common methods include Variational Bayes (e.g., Expectation-Maximization algorithm, as in the original paper) and Gibbs Sampling. These methods iteratively refine estimates of the latent variables until convergence, effectively "discovering" the topics.

## Algorithm Steps

1. Initialize the topic assignments for each word in each document randomly, or with a small amount of noise.
2. For each document $d$, initialize the variational parameters $\gamma_d$ (for $\theta_d$) and $\phi_{d,n}$ (for $z_{d,n}$) randomly.
3. Iteratively update $\phi_{d,n,k} \propto \exp(\Psi(\gamma_{d,k}) + \Psi(\lambda_{k,w_{d,n}}))$ for each word $w_{d,n}$ and topic $k$, where $\Psi$ is the digamma function.
4. Update $\gamma_{d,k} = \alpha_k + \sum_{n=1}^{N_d} \phi_{d,n,k}$ for each topic $k$.
5. After processing all documents, update the global variational parameters $\lambda_k$ (for $\phi_k$) based on the aggregated $\phi_{d,n}$ values from all documents.
6. Repeat the document-level and global parameter updates for a fixed number of iterations or until the lower bound on the log-likelihood converges.
7. Once converged, the document-topic distributions are approximated by $\theta_d \approx \text{Dir}(\gamma_d)$, and topic-word distributions by $\phi_k \approx \text{Dir}(\lambda_k)$.

## Parameters

| Parameter | Type | Default | Range / Options | Description |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `n_components` | `int` | `10` | > 0 | Number of topics to extract. |
| `doc_topic_prior` | `float or None` | `None` | > 0 | Prior of document topic distribution $\alpha$. If None, defaults to $1 / \text{n_components}$. |
| `topic_word_prior` | `float or None` | `None` | > 0 | Prior of topic word distribution $\eta$ (often denoted $\beta$). If None, defaults to $1 / \text{n_components}$. |
| `learning_method` | `str` | `'batch'` | 'batch', 'online' | Method used to update the variational parameters. 'batch' uses all training data at each iteration. 'online' uses mini-batches. |
| `max_iter` | `int` | `10` | > 0 | Maximum number of iterations. |
| `evaluate_every` | `int` | `10` | int >= 0 | How often to evaluate perplexity. Set to 0 to never evaluate. |
| `random_state` | `int or RandomState instance or None` | `None` | int >= 0 | Used for random initialization. |

## Strengths

- LDA provides an interpretable probabilistic framework for understanding the thematic structure of large text corpora.
- It can discover latent topics without requiring any prior labeling or knowledge of the document content.
- The model naturally handles polysemy (words with multiple meanings) and synonymy (multiple words with similar meanings) by associating them with different topics.
- LDA produces a soft clustering of documents, where each document can belong to multiple topics with varying degrees.
- The probabilistic nature allows for principled extensions and integration with other Bayesian models.
- It can be used for dimensionality reduction, transforming high-dimensional text data into a lower-dimensional topic space.

## Weaknesses

- The number of topics $K$ must be specified in advance, which can be challenging to determine optimally.
- LDA assumes that the order of words within a document does not matter (bag-of-words assumption), ignoring syntactic and semantic relationships.
- The computational cost for inference can be high, especially for very large corpora and a large number of topics.
- The quality of discovered topics can be sensitive to hyperparameter settings, particularly $\alpha$ and $\eta$.
- Topics discovered by LDA can sometimes be difficult to interpret or may overlap significantly.

## Use Cases

### Document Organization

Automatically categorizing and organizing large collections of articles, emails, or scientific papers into coherent themes.

### Information Retrieval

Enhancing search engines by allowing users to search for documents based on their thematic content rather than just keyword matching.

### Recommendation Systems

Suggesting articles, books, or products to users based on their inferred topic preferences from past interactions.

### Sentiment Analysis

Identifying dominant themes in customer reviews or social media posts to understand public opinion on products or services.

### Scientific Literature Analysis

Discovering emerging research trends, identifying influential papers, and mapping the intellectual landscape of a scientific field.

### Digital Humanities

Analyzing historical texts, literary works, or archival documents to uncover hidden patterns and thematic structures.
