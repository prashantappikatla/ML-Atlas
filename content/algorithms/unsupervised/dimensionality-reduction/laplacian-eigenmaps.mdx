## Overview

[Laplacian Eigenmaps](/algorithms/unsupervised/laplacian-eigenmaps) is a nonlinear dimensionality reduction technique that aims to preserve the local neighborhood structure of data points. It constructs a weighted graph representing the data manifold and then finds a low-dimensional embedding that minimizes the distortion of local proximities. This method is particularly effective for visualizing data that lies on a low-dimensional manifold embedded in a higher-dimensional space, making it a powerful tool for manifold learning.

## History

**2001** — Mikhail Belkin and Partha Niyogi introduce Laplacian Eigenmaps in their seminal paper "Laplacian Eigenmaps for [Dimensionality Reduction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dimensionality_reduction) and Data Representation," establishing a graph-based approach to nonlinear dimensionality reduction.

**2002** — The algorithm gains recognition as part of the broader field of manifold learning, alongside other techniques like [Isomap](/algorithms/unsupervised/isomap) and [Locally Linear Embedding](/algorithms/unsupervised/locally-linear-embedding) (LLE), which also focus on preserving geometric properties.

**2003** — Researchers begin exploring extensions and applications of Laplacian Eigenmaps, including its use in semi-supervised learning and [Spectral Clustering](/algorithms/unsupervised/spectral-clustering), leveraging its ability to capture data manifold structure.

**2007** — The theoretical connections between Laplacian Eigenmaps and other spectral methods, such as Spectral Clustering, are further elucidated, highlighting its versatility in various machine learning tasks.

**2010** — Laplacian Eigenmaps becomes a standard technique in manifold learning toolkits, influencing the development of more advanced graph-based methods for data visualization and feature extraction throughout the decade.

## Mathematical Intuition

### Constructing the Similarity Graph

Laplacian Eigenmaps begins by constructing a weighted undirected graph $G = (V, E)$ where each data point $x_i \in \mathbb{R}^D$ is a vertex. Edges are typically formed between $k$-nearest neighbors or points within an $\epsilon$-radius. The weight $w_{ij}$ of an edge between $x_i$ and $x_j$ quantifies their similarity, often using a Gaussian kernel: $$w_{ij} = e^{-\frac{||x_i - x_j||^2}{\sigma^2}}$$ where $\sigma$ is a scaling parameter. If $x_i$ and $x_j$ are not connected, $w_{ij} = 0$.

$$
G = (V, E)
$$

$$
x_i \in \mathbb{R}^D
$$

$$
w_{ij} = e^{-\frac{||x_i - x_j||^2}{\sigma^2}}
$$

### Graph Laplacian Definition

From the weight matrix $W = [w_{ij}]$, a diagonal degree matrix $D$ is constructed, where $D_{ii} = \sum_j w_{ij}$. The unnormalized Graph Laplacian $L$ is then defined as $L = D - W$. This matrix plays a central role in spectral graph theory, encoding the connectivity and structure of the graph.

$$
W = [w_{ij}]
$$

$$
D_{ii} = \sum_j w_{ij}
$$

$$
L = D - W
$$

### Minimizing Local Distances

The objective of Laplacian Eigenmaps is to find a low-dimensional embedding $y_i \in \mathbb{R}^d$ (where $d \ll D$) for each $x_i$ such that if $x_i$ and $x_j$ are close in the original space (i.e., $w_{ij}$ is large), then their images $y_i$ and $y_j$ are also close. This is formulated as minimizing the objective function: $$\min_{Y} \sum_{i,j} w_{ij} ||y_i - y_j||^2$$ This sum penalizes mapping connected points far apart. Using the Graph Laplacian, this objective can be rewritten in matrix form as $\text{Tr}(Y^T L Y)$, where $Y = [y_1, \dots, y_N]^T$ is the matrix of embedded points.

$$
y_i \in \mathbb{R}^d
$$

$$
d \ll D
$$

$$
\min_{Y} \sum_{i,j} w_{ij} ||y_i - y_j||^2
$$

$$
\text{Tr}(Y^T L Y)
$$

$$
Y = [y_1, \dots, y_N]^T
$$

### Generalized Eigenvalue Problem

To prevent trivial solutions (e.g., all $y_i = 0$) and ensure a unique scaling, constraints are added. A common constraint is $Y^T D Y = I$, which ensures that the embedding vectors are orthonormal with respect to the degree matrix. Minimizing $\text{Tr}(Y^T L Y)$ subject to $Y^T D Y = I$ leads to the generalized eigenvalue problem: $$Lv = \lambda Dv$$ The optimal low-dimensional embedding is formed by the eigenvectors corresponding to the smallest non-zero eigenvalues. The first eigenvector (corresponding to $\lambda_0 = 0$) is typically discarded as it represents a constant vector.

$$
Y^T D Y = I
$$

$$
Lv = \lambda Dv
$$

$$
\lambda_0 = 0
$$

## Algorithm Steps

1. Construct a similarity graph $G$ for the $N$ data points, connecting $x_i$ and $x_j$ if $x_j$ is among the $k$-nearest neighbors of $x_i$ (or vice versa), or if $||x_i - x_j|| < \epsilon$.
2. Compute the weight matrix $W$ for the graph, setting $w_{ij} = e^{-\frac{||x_i - x_j||^2}{\sigma^2}}$ for connected points and $w_{ij} = 0$ otherwise.
3. Compute the diagonal degree matrix $D$, where each diagonal element $D_{ii}$ is the sum of weights of edges connected to vertex $i$, i.e., $D_{ii} = \sum_j w_{ij}$.
4. Compute the unnormalized Graph Laplacian $L = D - W$.
5. Solve the generalized eigenvalue problem $Lv = \lambda Dv$ for its eigenvalues $\lambda$ and corresponding eigenvectors $v$.
6. Sort the eigenvalues in ascending order: $0 = \lambda_0 \le \lambda_1 \le \dots \le \lambda_{N-1}$.
7. Select the $d$ eigenvectors $v_1, \dots, v_d$ corresponding to the $d$ smallest non-zero eigenvalues $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_d$.
8. The low-dimensional embedding for data point $x_i$ is given by the $i$-th row of the matrix formed by these selected eigenvectors, $Y = [v_1, \dots, v_d]$. The $j$-th component of $y_i$ is $v_{j,i}$.

## Strengths

- Laplacian Eigenmaps effectively preserves the local neighborhood structure of the data, making it suitable for uncovering intrinsic manifold structures.
- The method is computationally efficient for sparse graphs, as the eigenvalue problem involves sparse matrices.
- It is robust to outliers compared to global methods like [PCA](/algorithms/unsupervised/pca), as its objective function focuses on local relationships.
- The algorithm provides a clear mathematical foundation rooted in spectral graph theory, offering interpretability of its embedding properties.
- It can discover non-linear structures in data that linear methods like PCA would fail to capture.

## Weaknesses

- The choice of neighborhood parameters (e.g., $k$ for k-NN graph, $\epsilon$ for $\epsilon$-neighborhood, or $\sigma$ for Gaussian kernel) significantly impacts the embedding quality and can be difficult to tune.
- Laplacian Eigenmaps does not inherently provide an out-of-sample mapping function, meaning new data points cannot be directly embedded without re-running the entire algorithm or using an approximation.
- The global structure of the data may not be well-preserved, as the objective function primarily focuses on local proximities.
- The computational cost of solving the generalized eigenvalue problem can be high for very large datasets, even with sparse matrices.

## Use Cases

### Bioinformatics

Used for visualizing gene expression data or protein interaction networks to identify clusters of related genes or proteins, revealing underlying biological patterns.

### Computer Vision

Applied to image manifold learning for tasks like face recognition, image retrieval, or dimensionality reduction of high-dimensional image features to improve processing efficiency.

### Natural Language Processing

Employed for visualizing word embeddings or document embeddings, helping to understand semantic relationships and cluster similar texts within a corpus.

### Social Network Analysis

Utilized to embed nodes in a social network into a low-dimensional space, revealing community structures, influential users, or patterns of interaction.

### Medical Imaging

Applied to reduce the dimensionality of fMRI or other medical scan data, aiding in the visualization of brain activity patterns or disease progression for diagnostic purposes.

### Robotics

Used for learning low-dimensional representations of robot sensor data or motor control states, facilitating more efficient navigation, control, and task learning.
