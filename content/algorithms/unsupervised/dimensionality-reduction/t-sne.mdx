## Overview

t-Distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding ([t-SNE](/algorithms/unsupervised/t-sne)) is a non-linear dimensionality reduction technique particularly well-suited for visualizing high-dimensional datasets. It maps high-dimensional data points to a low-dimensional space, typically two or three dimensions, such that similar points in the high-dimensional space are modeled by nearby points in the low-dimensional space, and dissimilar points are modeled by distant points. Unlike linear methods, t-SNE excels at preserving local structures within the data, making it highly effective for revealing clusters and intrinsic data manifolds.

## History

**2002** — [Geoffrey Hinton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geoffrey_Hinton) and Sam Roweis introduced Stochastic Neighbor Embedding (SNE), the precursor to t-SNE, which aimed to preserve neighborhood identities in a lower-dimensional space.

**2008** — Laurens van der Maaten and Geoffrey Hinton published "Visualizing Data using t-SNE," introducing the t-distribution in the low-dimensional space to alleviate the "crowding problem" of SNE.

**2014** — Laurens van der Maaten introduced Barnes-Hut t-SNE, an approximation that significantly speeds up computations for large datasets by using a quadtree or octree to approximate forces between distant points.

**2019** — George C. Linderman, et al., introduced FIt-SNE (Fast Interpolation-based t-SNE), further accelerating the algorithm by using interpolation to approximate the gradient, making it suitable for even larger datasets.

## Mathematical Intuition

### High-Dimensional Probabilities

In the high-dimensional space, t-SNE constructs a probability distribution over pairs of data points, where the probability $p_{j|i}$ that point $x_i$ picks point $x_j$ as its neighbor is given by a Gaussian distribution centered at $x_i$. 
$$p_{j|i} = \frac{\exp(-\|x_i - x_j\|^2 / (2\sigma_i^2))}{\sum_{k \neq i} \exp(-\|x_i - x_k\|^2 / (2\sigma_i^2))}$$
The bandwidth $\sigma_i$ of the Gaussian is set such that the perplexity of the conditional distribution equals a user-defined value. For symmetric probabilities, $p_{ij} = (p_{j|i} + p_{i|j}) / (2N)$.

$$
p_{j|i} = \frac{\exp(-\|x_i - x_j\|^2 / (2\sigma_i^2))}{\sum_{k \neq i} \exp(-\|x_i - x_k\|^2 / (2\sigma_i^2))}
$$

$$
p_{ij} = (p_{j|i} + p_{i|j}) / (2N)
$$

### Low-Dimensional Probabilities

In the low-dimensional space, t-SNE models the similarity between two points $y_i$ and $y_j$ using a Student's t-distribution with one degree of freedom (which is equivalent to a Cauchy distribution). This heavy-tailed distribution allows dissimilar points to be modeled by larger distances in the low-dimensional map, effectively mitigating the crowding problem. 
$$q_{ij} = \frac{(1 + \|y_i - y_j\|^2)^{-1}}{\sum_{k \neq l} (1 + \|y_k - y_l\|^2)^{-1}}$$

$$
q_{ij} = \frac{(1 + \|y_i - y_j\|^2)^{-1}}{\sum_{k \neq l} (1 + \|y_k - y_l\|^2)^{-1}}
$$

### Kullback-Leibler Divergence Objective

The objective of t-SNE is to minimize the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence between the high-dimensional probability distribution $P$ and the low-dimensional probability distribution $Q$. Minimizing this divergence ensures that the low-dimensional representation accurately reflects the neighborhood structure of the high-dimensional data. 
$$C = \sum_i \sum_j p_{ij} \log \frac{p_{ij}}{q_{ij}}$$
The gradient of the cost function with respect to the low-dimensional points $y_i$ is used for optimization.

$$
C = \sum_i \sum_j p_{ij} \log \frac{p_{ij}}{q_{ij}}
$$

### Gradient Descent Optimization

The low-dimensional points $Y = \{y_1, ..., y_N\}$ are initialized randomly and then optimized using gradient descent. The gradient update rule incorporates both attractive forces between points that are neighbors in high-dimensional space and repulsive forces between points that are not. 
$$\frac{\partial C}{\partial y_i} = 4 \sum_j (p_{ij} - q_{ij}) (y_i - y_j) (1 + \|y_i - y_j\|^2)^{-1}$$
An early exaggeration phase is often used to create tighter clusters, and [Momentum](/algorithms/meta-learning/momentum) is typically applied to accelerate convergence and avoid poor local minima.

$$
\frac{\partial C}{\partial y_i} = 4 \sum_j (p_{ij} - q_{ij}) (y_i - y_j) (1 + \|y_i - y_j\|^2)^{-1}
$$

## Algorithm Steps

1. For each high-dimensional data point $x_i$, compute the conditional probability $p_{j|i}$ that $x_i$ would pick $x_j$ as its neighbor, using a Gaussian distribution centered at $x_i$ with a bandwidth $\sigma_i$ determined by the user-defined perplexity.
2. Construct the symmetric pairwise probabilities $p_{ij} = (p_{j|i} + p_{i|j}) / (2N)$ for all pairs of points in the high-dimensional space.
3. Initialize the low-dimensional representation $Y = \{y_1, ..., y_N\}$ randomly, typically in two or three dimensions.
4. Compute the pairwise probabilities $q_{ij}$ in the low-dimensional space using a Student's t-distribution with one degree of freedom.
5. Minimize the Kullback-Leibler divergence $C = \sum_i \sum_j p_{ij} \log (p_{ij} / q_{ij})$ between the high-dimensional and low-dimensional probability distributions.
6. Iteratively update the low-dimensional points $y_i$ using gradient descent, applying an early exaggeration factor for the first few iterations to encourage the formation of well-separated clusters.
7. Continue the optimization process for a specified number of iterations, often incorporating momentum to stabilize and accelerate convergence.

## Parameters

| Parameter | Type | Default | Range / Options | Description |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `n_components` | `int` | `2` | [1, infinity) | Dimension of the embedded space. |
| `perplexity` | `float` | `30.0` | (1, N-1) | Related to the number of nearest neighbors used in the high-dimensional space. It balances attention between local and global aspects of the data. |
| `early_exaggeration` | `float` | `12.0` | (0, infinity) | Controls how tight natural clusters in the original space are permitted to be in the embedded space. A higher value increases the gaps between clusters. |
| `learning_rate` | `float` | `200.0` | (0, infinity) | The learning rate for the gradient descent optimization. |
| `n_iter` | `int` | `1000` | (250, infinity) | Maximum number of iterations for the optimization. |
| `metric` | `str` | `'euclidean'` | {'euclidean', 'manhattan', 'cosine', ...} | The metric to use when calculating distance between instances in a feature array. |
| `init` | `str` | `'pca'` | {'random', 'pca'} or a numpy.ndarray | Initialization of the embedding. 'pca' is recommended for dense data, 'random' for sparse data. |
| `random_state` | `int or RandomState instance or None` | `None` | [0, infinity) or None | Determines the random number generator seed for reproducibility. |

## Strengths

- It excels at visualizing high-dimensional data by preserving local structures and revealing clusters that might be obscured by linear methods.
- The use of a heavy-tailed Student's t-distribution in the low-dimensional space effectively mitigates the "crowding problem," allowing for better separation of clusters.
- t-SNE can uncover complex, non-linear relationships within data, making it valuable for exploratory data analysis.
- The perplexity parameter offers a flexible way to balance attention between local and global aspects of the data structure.
- It produces visually intuitive and interpretable maps, often showing distinct, well-separated clusters.

## Weaknesses

- The computational complexity of t-SNE is high, scaling as $O(N^2)$ or $O(N \log N)$ with approximations, making it slow for very large datasets.
- t-SNE is sensitive to its hyperparameters, particularly perplexity and early exaggeration, which can significantly influence the resulting visualization.
- The global structure of the data is not always well-preserved, meaning distances between widely separated clusters in the t-SNE plot may not accurately reflect their true distances in high-dimensional space.
- t-SNE is a non-deterministic algorithm; different runs with different random initializations can produce different visualizations, making reproducibility challenging without fixing the random state.
- It is primarily a visualization tool and does not provide a direct mapping function for new data points, requiring re-computation for new samples.

## Use Cases

### Bioinformatics

Visualizing gene expression data, single-cell RNA sequencing data, or protein interaction networks to identify cell types or disease states.

### Image Processing

Exploring feature vectors extracted from images to understand image similarity, identify object categories, or detect anomalies.

### Natural Language Processing

Visualizing word embeddings or document embeddings to understand semantic relationships between words or cluster documents by topic.

### Cybersecurity

Detecting anomalies in network traffic data or user behavior logs by visualizing high-dimensional feature vectors to identify unusual patterns.

### Customer Segmentation

Grouping customers based on their purchasing behavior or demographic data to identify distinct market segments.

### Quality Control

Visualizing manufacturing sensor data to identify clusters corresponding to different operational states or defect types.
