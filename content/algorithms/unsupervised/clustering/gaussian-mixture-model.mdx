## Overview

[Gaussian Mixture Models](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mixture_model#Gaussian_mixture_model) (GMMs) are probabilistic models that assume all data points are generated from a mixture of a finite number of Gaussian distributions with unknown parameters. They are used for density estimation and clustering, providing a more flexible approach than [K-Means](/algorithms/unsupervised/k-means) by assigning probabilities of belonging to each cluster rather than hard assignments. GMMs model the underlying distribution of the data, allowing for clusters of varying sizes, shapes, and orientations. The Expectation-Maximization (EM) algorithm is typically employed to estimate the model parameters.

## History

**1960** — Early theoretical work on statistical mixture models, including Gaussian mixtures, began to emerge, laying the groundwork for probabilistic clustering and density estimation.

**1965** — John H. Wolfe published "A Computer Program for the Maximum Likelihood Analysis of Types," demonstrating practical application of maximum likelihood estimation to multivariate normal mixtures.

**1977** — Arthur P. Dempster, Nan M. Laird, and Donald B. Rubin formalized the Expectation-Maximization (EM) algorithm in their seminal paper "Maximum Likelihood from Incomplete Data via the EM Algorithm," providing a general iterative method for fitting GMMs.

**1980** — GMMs gained significant traction in fields such as speech recognition, where they were used to model the statistical properties of speech signals.

**1990** — Further research and computational advancements led to the widespread adoption of GMMs in various machine learning applications, including image processing and bioinformatics.

**2000** — Increased computational power and algorithmic refinements led to the integration of GMMs into standard machine learning libraries, making them accessible for general-purpose clustering and density estimation tasks.

## Mathematical Intuition

### Mixture Model Formulation

A [Gaussian Mixture Model](/algorithms/unsupervised/gaussian-mixture-model) assumes that the data $x$ is generated from a weighted sum of $K$ Gaussian distributions. The probability density function of a GMM for a data point $x$ is given by:
$$p(x | \theta) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \pi_k \mathcal{N}(x | \mu_k, \Sigma_k)$$
Here, $\pi_k$ are the mixing coefficients (or prior probabilities) for each component $k$, satisfying $\sum_{k=1}^{K} \pi_k = 1$ and $\pi_k \ge 0$. $\mu_k$ are the mean vectors, and $\Sigma_k$ are the covariance matrices for each component $k$. The parameters $\theta = \{(\pi_k, \mu_k, \Sigma_k)\}_{k=1}^K$ are estimated from the data. The probability density function for a multivariate Gaussian distribution is:
$$\mathcal{N}(x | \mu, \Sigma) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{(2\pi)^D |\Sigma|}} \exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}(x - \mu)^T \Sigma^{-1}(x - \mu)\right)$$
where $D$ is the dimensionality of the data.

$$
p(x | \theta) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \pi_k \mathcal{N}(x | \mu_k, \Sigma_k)
$$

$$
\mathcal{N}(x | \mu, \Sigma) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{(2\pi)^D |\Sigma|}} \exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}(x - \mu)^T \Sigma^{-1}(x - \mu)\right)
$$

### Log-Likelihood Function

Given a dataset $X = \{x_1, ..., x_N\}$, the objective is to find the parameters $\theta$ that maximize the log-likelihood of the observed data. The log-likelihood function is:
$$\log p(X | \theta) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \log \left( \sum_{k=1}^{K} \pi_k \mathcal{N}(x_i | \mu_k, \Sigma_k) \right)$$
Directly maximizing this function is analytically intractable due to the sum inside the logarithm. The Expectation-Maximization (EM) algorithm provides an iterative approach to find a local maximum of this function by introducing latent variables.

$$
\log p(X | \theta) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \log \left( \sum_{k=1}^{K} \pi_k \mathcal{N}(x_i | \mu_k, \Sigma_k) \right)
$$

### E-Step: Compute Responsibilities

In the Expectation (E) step, we compute the posterior probability that each data point $x_i$ belongs to each component $k$, given the current parameter estimates $\theta^{\text{old}}$. These posterior probabilities are often referred to as responsibilities, denoted by $\gamma(z_{ik})$. The latent variable $z_{ik}$ is 1 if $x_i$ was generated by component $k$, and 0 otherwise. The responsibility is calculated using Bayes' theorem:
$$\gamma(z_{ik}) = p(z_{ik}=1 | x_i, \theta^{\text{old}}) = \frac{\pi_k^{\text{old}} \mathcal{N}(x_i | \mu_k^{\text{old}}, \Sigma_k^{\text{old}})}{\sum_{j=1}^{K} \pi_j^{\text{old}} \mathcal{N}(x_i | \mu_j^{\text{old}}, \Sigma_j^{\text{old}})}$$

$$
\gamma(z_{ik}) = p(z_{ik}=1 | x_i, \theta^{\text{old}}) = \frac{\pi_k^{\text{old}} \mathcal{N}(x_i | \mu_k^{\text{old}}, \Sigma_k^{\text{old}})}{\sum_{j=1}^{K} \pi_j^{\text{old}} \mathcal{N}(x_i | \mu_j^{\text{old}}, \Sigma_j^{\text{old}})}
$$

### M-Step: Update Parameters

In the Maximization (M) step, we update the model parameters $\pi_k$, $\mu_k$, and $\Sigma_k$ by maximizing the expected complete-data log-likelihood, using the responsibilities $\gamma(z_{ik})$ computed in the E-step. First, the effective number of points assigned to component $k$ is calculated as $N_k = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \gamma(z_{ik})$. The updated parameters are:
Updated means:
$$\mu_k^{\text{new}} = \frac{1}{N_k} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \gamma(z_{ik}) x_i$$
Updated covariance matrices:
$$\Sigma_k^{\text{new}} = \frac{1}{N_k} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \gamma(z_{ik}) (x_i - \mu_k^{\text{new}}) (x_i - \mu_k^{\text{new}})^T$$
Updated mixing coefficients:
$$\pi_k^{\text{new}} = \frac{N_k}{N}$$
These steps are iterated until convergence.

$$
N_k = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \gamma(z_{ik})
$$

$$
\mu_k^{\text{new}} = \frac{1}{N_k} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \gamma(z_{ik}) x_i
$$

$$
\Sigma_k^{\text{new}} = \frac{1}{N_k} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \gamma(z_{ik}) (x_i - \mu_k^{\text{new}}) (x_i - \mu_k^{\text{new}})^T
$$

$$
\pi_k^{\text{new}} = \frac{N_k}{N}
$$

## Algorithm Steps

1. Initialize the parameters: mixing coefficients $\pi_k$, means $\mu_k$, and covariance matrices $\Sigma_k$ for each of the $K$ Gaussian components, often using K-Means or random initialization.
2. **E-step (Expectation)**: For each data point $x_i$ and each component $k$, calculate the responsibility $\gamma(z_{ik})$ that component $k$ takes for $x_i$, using the current parameter estimates: $\gamma(z_{ik}) = \frac{\pi_k \mathcal{N}(x_i | \mu_k, \Sigma_k)}{\sum_{j=1}^{K} \pi_j \mathcal{N}(x_i | \mu_j, \Sigma_j)}$.
3. Calculate the effective number of points for each component: $N_k = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \gamma(z_{ik})$.
4. **M-step (Maximization)**: Update the means for each component: $\mu_k^{\text{new}} = \frac{1}{N_k} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \gamma(z_{ik}) x_i$.
5. Update the covariance matrices for each component: $\Sigma_k^{\text{new}} = \frac{1}{N_k} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \gamma(z_{ik}) (x_i - \mu_k^{\text{new}}) (x_i - \mu_k^{\text{new}})^T$.
6. Update the mixing coefficients for each component: $\pi_k^{\text{new}} = \frac{N_k}{N}$.
7. Check for convergence: Repeat the E-step and M-step until the change in the log-likelihood function or the parameters falls below a predefined tolerance, or a maximum number of iterations is reached.

## Parameters

| Parameter | Type | Default | Range / Options | Description |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `n_components` | `int` | `1` | integer ≥ 1 | The number of mixture components. |
| `covariance_type` | `str` | `'full'` | 'full', 'tied', 'diag', 'spherical' | Controls the type of covariance matrix for each component: 'full' (each component has its own general covariance matrix), 'tied' (all components share the same general covariance matrix), 'diag' (each component has its own diagonal covariance matrix), 'spherical' (each component has its own single variance). |
| `tol` | `float` | `1e-3` | float ≥ 0 | The convergence threshold. EM iterations will stop when the lower bound average gain is below this threshold. |
| `max_iter` | `int` | `100` | integer ≥ 1 | The maximum number of EM iterations to perform. |
| `random_state` | `int, RandomState instance or None` | `None` | integer, RandomState instance, or None | Controls the pseudo random number generator used for random initialization. Pass an int for reproducible output across multiple function calls. |
| `init_params` | `str` | `'kmeans'` | 'kmeans', 'random' | The method used to initialize the weights, means and precisions. 'kmeans' uses K-Means to initialize means, 'random' uses random samples. |
| `weights_init` | `array-like of shape (n_components,)` | `None` | array of floats, sum to 1 | The user-provided initial weights. If None, weights are initialized using `init_params`. |
| `means_init` | `array-like of shape (n_components, n_features)` | `None` | array of floats | The user-provided initial means. If None, means are initialized using `init_params`. |
| `precisions_init` | `array-like` | `None` | array of floats, depends on `covariance_type` | The user-provided initial precisions (inverse of covariance matrices). If None, precisions are initialized using `init_params`. |

## Strengths

- GMMs can model clusters of arbitrary elliptical shapes and orientations, providing more flexibility than K-Means which assumes spherical clusters.
- They provide a probabilistic assignment of data points to clusters, offering soft memberships rather than hard assignments.
- GMMs can be used for density estimation, providing a generative model of the underlying data distribution.
- The EM algorithm is guaranteed to converge to a local maximum of the likelihood function.
- The model naturally handles overlapping clusters by assigning fractional memberships to data points.
- It can detect outliers by identifying points with very low probability under the learned mixture model.

## Weaknesses

- GMMs are sensitive to initialization and can converge to local optima, often requiring multiple restarts or careful initialization strategies.
- Determining the optimal number of components $K$ is challenging and typically requires external criteria like AIC or BIC.
- The algorithm can be computationally expensive for high-dimensional data or a large number of components due to matrix inversions.
- GMMs assume that the underlying data distribution is a mixture of Gaussians, which may not always hold true for real-world data.
- The model can be prone to overfitting, especially when the number of components is too high relative to the data size, potentially leading to singular covariance matrices.

## Use Cases

### Customer Segmentation

Identifying distinct groups of customers based on purchasing behavior, demographics, or interaction patterns for targeted marketing campaigns.

### Anomaly Detection

Flagging unusual data points in system logs, network traffic, or sensor readings that have very low probability under the learned normal data distribution.

### Image Segmentation

Separating different regions or objects within an image by modeling pixel intensities or color features as a mixture of Gaussians to delineate distinct areas.

### Speech Recognition

Modeling phonemes or speech units as Gaussian mixtures, where each component represents a different characteristic sound, for robust speech processing.

### Bioinformatics

Clustering gene expression data to identify groups of genes with similar functions or regulatory patterns, aiding in disease classification or biological discovery.

### Financial Modeling

Identifying different market regimes or states based on financial time series data, which can inform trading strategies or risk management.
