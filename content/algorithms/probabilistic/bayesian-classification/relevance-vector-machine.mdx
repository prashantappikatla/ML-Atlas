## Overview

The [Relevance Vector Machine](/algorithms/probabilistic/relevance-vector-machine) (RVM) is a sparse Bayesian kernel-based model for regression and classification tasks. It leverages a hierarchical Bayesian framework with an Automatic Relevance Determination (ARD) prior over model weights, which promotes sparsity by driving many weights to zero. This results in a model that typically uses fewer basis functions (relevance vectors) than [Support Vector Machines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Support_vector_machine), while providing probabilistic predictions.

## History

**2001** — Michael E. Tipping introduced the Relevance Vector Machine in his seminal paper, 'Sparse Bayesian Learning and the Relevance Vector Machine,' establishing a new paradigm for sparse kernel models.

**2003** — Tipping's work was further detailed and expanded in his Ph.D. thesis, solidifying the theoretical foundations and practical implications of RVMs for various machine learning problems.

**2004** — The RVM was included in Christopher Bishop's influential textbook 'Pattern Recognition and [Machine Learning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Machine_learning),' increasing its visibility and adoption within the academic community as a powerful alternative to SVMs.

**2006** — Extensions and improvements to the RVM algorithm, such as faster optimization methods and handling of larger datasets, began to emerge, addressing some of its computational challenges.

## Mathematical Intuition

### RVM Model and Likelihood

The RVM model assumes a functional form similar to a generalized linear model, where the output $y(\mathbf{x})$ is a linear combination of kernel functions. For regression, the target $t$ for an input $\mathbf{x}$ is modeled as $t = y(\mathbf{x}) + \epsilon$, where $\epsilon$ is Gaussian noise with variance $\sigma^2$. The model output is given by: $$y(\mathbf{x}; \mathbf{w}) = \sum_{i=1}^N w_i K(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}_i) + w_0$$ where $K(\cdot, \cdot)$ is a kernel function, $\mathbf{x}_i$ are the training inputs, and $\mathbf{w} = [w_0, w_1, \dots, w_N]^T$ are the weights. The likelihood of the data $\mathbf{t} = [t_1, \dots, t_N]^T$ given the weights and noise variance is: $$p(\mathbf{t}|\mathbf{w}, \sigma^2) = \prod_{n=1}^N \mathcal{N}(t_n|y(\mathbf{x}_n; \mathbf{w}), \sigma^2)$$ For classification, a sigmoid function is typically applied to $y(\mathbf{x})$ to model the probability of class membership.

$$
y(\mathbf{x}; \mathbf{w}) = \sum_{i=1}^N w_i K(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}_i) + w_0
$$

$$
p(\mathbf{t}|\mathbf{w}, \sigma^2) = \prod_{n=1}^N \mathcal{N}(t_n|y(\mathbf{x}_n; \mathbf{w}), \sigma^2)
$$

### Bayesian Priors and Sparsity

To achieve sparsity and avoid overfitting, RVM employs a hierarchical Bayesian prior over the weights $\mathbf{w}$. Each weight $w_i$ is assigned an independent Gaussian prior with its own precision (inverse variance) parameter $\alpha_i$: $$p(\mathbf{w}|\boldsymbol{\alpha}) = \prod_{i=0}^N \mathcal{N}(w_i|0, \alpha_i^{-1})$$ where $\boldsymbol{\alpha} = [\alpha_0, \alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_N]^T$. These $\alpha_i$ are themselves given Gamma hyperpriors. During optimization, many $\alpha_i$ values tend to infinity, which in turn forces the corresponding $w_i$ to zero. The basis functions associated with non-zero weights are called 'relevance vectors,' analogous to support vectors in SVMs, but typically far fewer in number.

$$
p(\mathbf{w}|\boldsymbol{\alpha}) = \prod_{i=0}^N \mathcal{N}(w_i|0, \alpha_i^{-1})
$$

### Hyperparameter Optimization (ARD)

The key to RVM's performance lies in learning the hyperparameters $\boldsymbol{\alpha}$ and $\sigma^2$ from the data. This is typically done by maximizing the marginal likelihood (also known as evidence) $p(\mathbf{t}|\boldsymbol{\alpha}, \sigma^2)$, which is obtained by integrating out the weights $\mathbf{w}$: $$p(\mathbf{t}|\boldsymbol{\alpha}, \sigma^2) = \int p(\mathbf{t}|\mathbf{w}, \sigma^2) p(\mathbf{w}|\boldsymbol{\alpha}) d\mathbf{w}$$ This integral can be computed analytically due to the Gaussian nature of the likelihood and prior. The maximization is usually performed using an iterative Expectation-Maximization (EM) like procedure or a fast marginal likelihood maximization algorithm. In each iteration, the posterior distribution over $\mathbf{w}$ is computed, and then $\boldsymbol{\alpha}$ and $\sigma^2$ are updated based on the moments of this posterior.

$$
p(\mathbf{t}|\boldsymbol{\alpha}, \sigma^2) = \int p(\mathbf{t}|\mathbf{w}, \sigma^2) p(\mathbf{w}|\boldsymbol{\alpha}) d\mathbf{w}
$$

### Predictive Distribution

For a new input $\mathbf{x}^*$, the RVM provides a full predictive distribution $p(t^*|\mathbf{t}, \boldsymbol{\alpha}, \sigma^2)$, rather than just a point estimate. This is achieved by integrating over the posterior distribution of the weights: $$p(t^*|\mathbf{t}, \boldsymbol{\alpha}, \sigma^2) = \int p(t^*|\mathbf{x}^*, \mathbf{w}, \sigma^2) p(\mathbf{w}|\mathbf{t}, \boldsymbol{\alpha}, \sigma^2) d\mathbf{w}$$ For regression, this predictive distribution is Gaussian, with a mean and variance that depend on the relevance vectors and the learned hyperparameters. This provides a measure of uncertainty associated with each prediction, which is a significant advantage over point-estimate models.

$$
p(t^*|\mathbf{t}, \boldsymbol{\alpha}, \sigma^2) = \int p(t^*|\mathbf{x}^*, \mathbf{w}, \sigma^2) p(\mathbf{w}|\mathbf{t}, \boldsymbol{\alpha}, \sigma^2) d\mathbf{w}
$$

## Algorithm Steps

1. Initialize the hyperparameters $\boldsymbol{\alpha}$ and $\sigma^2$ to small positive values, and construct the kernel matrix $\mathbf{K}$ where $K_{ij} = K(\mathbf{x}_i, \mathbf{x}_j)$.
2. Iteratively update the posterior distribution over weights $\mathbf{w}$ given the current hyperparameters, calculating its mean $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ and covariance $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$.
3. Update the noise variance $\sigma^2$ using the current posterior moments of $\mathbf{w}$ and the data, typically via an analytical expression derived from maximizing the marginal likelihood.
4. Update each precision hyperparameter $\alpha_i$ based on the corresponding posterior mean $\mu_i$ and variance $\Sigma_{ii}$ of $w_i$, using an analytical update rule.
5. Monitor the $\alpha_i$ values; if an $\alpha_i$ becomes very large, the corresponding weight $w_i$ is effectively driven to zero, and that basis function (and its associated data point) can be removed from the model.
6. Repeat steps 2-5 until convergence, which is typically determined by a small change in the marginal likelihood or the hyperparameters between iterations, or until a maximum number of iterations is reached.
7. Upon convergence, the final set of non-zero weights $\mathbf{w}$ and their associated data points (relevance vectors) define the sparse model.
8. For prediction, use the learned posterior distribution of $\mathbf{w}$ and $\sigma^2$ to compute the predictive mean and variance for new input data points.

## Parameters

| Parameter | Type | Default | Range / Options | Description |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `kernel` | `str` | `rbf` | 'linear', 'poly', 'rbf', 'sigmoid' | Specifies the kernel type to be used in the algorithm. Common choices include 'linear', 'poly', 'rbf' (Gaussian), and 'sigmoid'. |
| `gamma` | `float or 'scale'` | `'scale'` | positive float or 'scale' | Kernel coefficient for 'rbf', 'poly', and 'sigmoid' kernels. If 'scale', it uses $1 / (n_{features} * X.var())$ as value. |
| `degree` | `int` | `3` | integer ≥ 1 | Degree of the polynomial kernel function ('poly'). Ignored by other kernels. |
| `coef0` | `float` | `0.0` | float | Independent term in kernel function. It is only significant in 'poly' and 'sigmoid' kernels. |
| `tol` | `float` | `1e-3` | positive float | Tolerance for stopping criterion. The algorithm converges when the change in hyperparameters falls below this threshold. |
| `max_iter` | `int` | `1000` | integer ≥ 1 | Maximum number of iterations for the optimization algorithm. If the algorithm does not converge within this limit, it stops. |

## Strengths

- RVMs typically produce much sparser models than Support Vector Machines, requiring fewer basis functions (relevance vectors) for prediction.
- The Bayesian framework provides probabilistic outputs for predictions, including a measure of uncertainty, which is valuable for decision-making.
- RVMs naturally avoid the need for a regularization parameter like SVM's $C$, as sparsity is achieved through the Automatic Relevance Determination prior.
- The model is less sensitive to the choice of kernel parameters compared to SVMs, as these are learned from the data.
- RVMs can generalize well even with limited training data due to their Bayesian formulation and inherent sparsity.

## Weaknesses

- The training process for RVMs can be computationally more expensive and slower than SVMs, especially for large datasets, due to the iterative optimization of hyperparameters.
- The optimization procedure for RVMs can converge to local optima, potentially leading to suboptimal models.
- RVMs can be sensitive to the choice of kernel function, and an inappropriate kernel may lead to poor performance.
- The probabilistic output for classification tasks often requires approximation methods, which can add complexity and potential inaccuracies.
- Unlike SVMs, RVMs do not have a guaranteed global optimum for the hyperparameter optimization, making convergence properties less predictable.

## Use Cases

### Medical Diagnosis

RVMs can be used for classifying diseases based on patient data, providing not only a diagnosis but also a confidence score, which is crucial in clinical settings.

### Bioinformatics

Applied in gene expression analysis or protein structure prediction, RVMs can identify key features (relevance vectors) that are most indicative of biological outcomes.

### Signal Processing

RVMs are effective for noise reduction, signal reconstruction, and system identification, particularly when dealing with sparse or high-dimensional sensor data.

### Financial Forecasting

Used for predicting stock prices or market trends, RVMs can offer probabilistic forecasts and identify the most relevant economic indicators.

### Robotics

In control systems and sensor fusion, RVMs can model complex dynamics and make robust predictions with uncertainty estimates, aiding in autonomous navigation and decision-making.

### Image Processing

After feature extraction, RVMs can be used for object recognition, image classification, or segmentation tasks, benefiting from their sparsity and probabilistic outputs.
