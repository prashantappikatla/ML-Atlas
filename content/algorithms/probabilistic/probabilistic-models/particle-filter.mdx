## Overview

Particle Filters are a class of sequential Monte Carlo methods used for estimating the state of a dynamic system over time, particularly when the system dynamics or measurement models are non-linear or non-Gaussian. They approximate the posterior probability distribution of the state using a set of weighted random samples, called particles, which are propagated and re-sampled over time. This approach provides a powerful non-parametric solution to the Bayesian filtering problem, offering flexibility beyond the limitations of linear-Gaussian assumptions.

## History

**1993** — Neil Gordon, David Salmond, and Adrian Smith published "Novel approach to nonlinear/non-Gaussian Bayesian state estimation," which is widely recognized as the seminal work introducing the modern [Particle Filter](/algorithms/probabilistic/particle-filter), also known as the Sequential Importance Resampling (SIR) filter.

**1996** — Genshiro Kitagawa introduced the "Monte Carlo filter and smoother for non-Gaussian nonlinear state space models," further developing the theoretical foundations and practical applications of these methods.

**2002** — Arnaud Doucet, Nando de Freitas, and Neil Gordon edited "Sequential Monte Carlo Methods in Practice," a comprehensive book that solidified the theoretical framework and practical applications of particle filters and related SMC techniques.

## Mathematical Intuition

### Bayesian Filtering Problem

The core problem is to estimate the hidden state $x_t$ of a dynamic system at time $t$, given a sequence of noisy observations $y_{1:t}$. This is typically formulated within a state-space model:
$$x_t = f(x_{t-1}, v_t)$$
$$y_t = h(x_t, w_t)$$
where $f$ is the state transition function, $h$ is the measurement function, and $v_t, w_t$ are process and measurement noise, respectively. The goal is to compute the posterior distribution $p(x_t | y_{1:t})$, known as the filtering distribution, using Bayes' theorem:
$$p(x_t | y_{1:t}) \propto p(y_t | x_t) \int p(x_t | x_{t-1}) p(x_{t-1} | y_{1:t-1}) dx_{t-1}$$
This integral is often intractable for non-linear or non-Gaussian systems.

$$

### Sequential Importance Resampling (SIR)

Particle filters approximate the filtering distribution $p(x_t | y_{1:t})$ by a set of $N$ weighted particles $\{x_t^{(i)}, w_t^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^N$. Each particle $x_t^{(i)}$ is a sample from the state space, and its associated weight $w_t^{(i)}$ represents the probability of that particle being drawn from the true posterior. The approximation is given by:
$$p(x_t | y_{1:t}) \approx \sum_{i=1}^N w_t^{(i)} \delta(x_t - x_t^{(i)})$$
where $\delta(\cdot)$ is the Dirac delta function. The SIR algorithm propagates these particles through time, updating their weights based on new observations and periodically resampling them to mitigate particle degeneracy.

### Weight Update Equation

At each time step $t$, after propagating particles from $x_{t-1}^{(i)}$ to $x_t^{(i)}$ using the state transition model $p(x_t | x_{t-1})$, the weights are updated based on the new observation $y_t$. The importance weight for each particle $i$ is typically calculated as:
$$w_t^{(i)} \propto w_{t-1}^{(i)} \frac{p(y_t | x_t^{(i)}) p(x_t^{(i)} | x_{t-1}^{(i)})}{q(x_t^{(i)} | x_{t-1}^{(i)}, y_t)}$$
where $q(\cdot)$ is the importance sampling distribution. In the common bootstrap filter (a type of SIR filter), the importance distribution is chosen as the state transition prior $q(x_t^{(i)} | x_{t-1}^{(i)}, y_t) = p(x_t^{(i)} | x_{t-1}^{(i)})$. This simplifies the weight update to:
$$w_t^{(i)} \propto w_{t-1}^{(i)} p(y_t | x_t^{(i)})$$
These weights are then normalized such that $\sum_{i=1}^N w_t^{(i)} = 1$.

### Resampling to Avoid Degeneracy

Over time, the variance of the importance weights $w_t^{(i)}$ can increase, leading to a situation where only a few particles have significant weights, while most have negligible weights. This phenomenon is known as particle degeneracy or impoverishment. To combat this, a resampling step is introduced. When the effective number of particles, $N_{eff} = 1 / \sum_{i=1}^N (w_t^{(i)})^2$, falls below a threshold, a new set of $N$ particles is drawn from the current set $\{x_t^{(i)}\}$ with probabilities proportional to their weights $w_t^{(i)}$. The new particles are then assigned equal weights $1/N$. Common resampling schemes include multinomial, stratified, and systematic resampling.

## Algorithm Steps

1. 1. Initialization: Sample $N$ particles $\{x_0^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^N$ from the initial state distribution $p(x_0)$ and assign them equal weights $w_0^{(i)} = 1/N$.
2. 2. Prediction (Propagation): For each particle $x_{t-1}^{(i)}$ from the previous time step, sample a new state $x_t^{(i)}$ from the state transition probability $p(x_t | x_{t-1}^{(i)})$.
3. 3. Weight Update: For each new particle $x_t^{(i)}$, calculate its importance weight $w_t^{(i)}$ based on the likelihood of the current observation $y_t$ given the particle's state: $w_t^{(i)} \propto w_{t-1}^{(i)} p(y_t | x_t^{(i)})$.
4. 4. Normalization: Normalize the weights such that $\sum_{i=1}^N w_t^{(i)} = 1$.
5. 5. Resampling: If the effective number of particles $N_{eff}$ is below a threshold, resample $N$ new particles from the current set $\{x_t^{(i)}\}$ with probabilities proportional to their normalized weights, then assign new equal weights $1/N$ to the resampled particles.
6. 6. State Estimation: The estimated state $\hat{x}_t$ can be computed as a weighted average of the particles: $\hat{x}_t = \sum_{i=1}^N w_t^{(i)} x_t^{(i)}$.
7. 7. Iteration: Repeat steps 2-6 for each subsequent time step $t+1$.

## Parameters

| Parameter | Type | Default | Range / Options | Description |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `n_particles` | `integer` | `100` | integer > 0 | The number of particles used to approximate the posterior distribution. A higher number generally leads to better accuracy but increased computational cost. |
| `resampling_strategy` | `string` | `multinomial` | ['multinomial', 'stratified', 'systematic', 'residual'] | The method used for resampling particles to combat degeneracy. Common options include 'multinomial', 'stratified', 'systematic', or 'residual'. |
| `noise_covariance_process` | `array-like or float` | `None` | positive definite matrix or positive float | The covariance matrix or scalar variance of the process noise $v_t$ in the state transition model. |
| `noise_covariance_measurement` | `array-like or float` | `None` | positive definite matrix or positive float | The covariance matrix or scalar variance of the measurement noise $w_t$ in the observation model. |
| `initial_state_distribution` | `callable or array-like` | `None` | callable returning samples or array of initial states | A function or distribution from which initial particles $x_0^{(i)}$ are sampled, representing the prior belief about the initial state. |
| `resampling_threshold` | `float` | `0.5` | 0.0 to 1.0 | The threshold for the effective number of particles ($N_{eff}$) below which resampling is triggered. Typically a fraction of `n_particles`. |

## Strengths

- Particle filters can effectively handle highly non-linear and non-Gaussian system dynamics and measurement models, unlike Kalman filters.
- They provide a full approximation of the posterior probability distribution of the state, not just point estimates or Gaussian approximations.
- The algorithm is conceptually straightforward and can be implemented with relative ease for many problems.
- Particle filters are inherently parallelizable, as each particle's propagation and weight update can be computed independently.
- They are robust to outliers in measurements, provided the likelihood function $p(y_t | x_t)$ is appropriately chosen.
- The method is flexible and can incorporate various noise models and complex system dynamics.

## Weaknesses

- The computational cost increases linearly with the number of particles, which can be prohibitive for high-dimensional state spaces.
- Particle degeneracy, where most particles have negligible weights, can lead to poor approximations if not addressed by effective resampling.
- The "curse of dimensionality" significantly impacts performance, as the number of particles required to adequately cover the state space grows exponentially with its dimension.
- Choosing an appropriate proposal distribution for importance sampling is crucial for efficiency but can be challenging in practice.
- Particle filters can struggle with multimodal posterior distributions if the number of particles is insufficient to represent all modes.

## Use Cases

### Robotics

Used for simultaneous localization and mapping (SLAM), robot localization, and tracking moving objects in dynamic environments.

### Computer Vision

Applied in object tracking, human pose estimation, and visual surveillance systems, especially in cluttered or occluded scenes.

### Signal Processing

Employed for target tracking in radar and sonar systems, audio source separation, and channel equalization in communication systems.

### Finance

Utilized for option pricing, risk management, and modeling stochastic volatility in financial markets.

### Epidemiology

Applied to estimate and forecast disease spread, track infection rates, and model population dynamics.

### Autonomous Driving

Essential for vehicle localization, tracking other vehicles and pedestrians, and sensor fusion.
