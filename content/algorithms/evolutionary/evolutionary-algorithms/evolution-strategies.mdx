## Overview

[Evolution Strategies](/algorithms/evolutionary/evolution-strategies) (ES) are a class of population-based, gradient-free optimization algorithms inspired by biological evolution. They are particularly effective for optimizing complex, non-differentiable, or black-box objective functions where traditional gradient-based methods are inapplicable. ES operates by iteratively generating a population of candidate solutions, perturbing them with random mutations, and selecting the fittest individuals to form the next generation. This approach allows for robust global optimization without requiring explicit gradient information.

## History

**1965** — Ingo Rechenberg introduced Evolution Strategies at the Technical University of Berlin for optimizing aerodynamic shapes, marking the genesis of the field.

**1970** — Hans-Paul Schwefel further developed ES, formalizing the $(\mu, \lambda)$ and $(\mu + \lambda)$ selection schemes and introducing self-adaptation of mutation parameters.

**1996** — The development of Covariance Matrix Adaptation Evolution Strategy (CMA-ES) by Nikolaus Hansen and Andreas Ostermeier significantly improved ES's performance on high-dimensional and ill-conditioned problems by adapting the full covariance matrix of the mutation distribution.

**2017** — OpenAI demonstrated the effectiveness of Evolution Strategies for deep reinforcement learning tasks, achieving competitive results with traditional policy gradient methods but with significantly higher parallelizability.

## Mathematical Intuition

### Population-based Search

Evolution Strategies maintain a population of $\mu$ parent solutions, each represented as a vector of parameters $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^D$. In each generation, $\lambda$ offspring solutions are generated by perturbing the parent solutions. The goal is to find $\mathbf{x}^*$ that minimizes (or maximizes) an objective function $f(\mathbf{x})$. A basic mutation step for an individual $\mathbf{x}_k$ to produce an offspring $\mathbf{x}_{k+1}$ can be expressed as:
$$ \mathbf{x}_{k+1} = \mathbf{x}_k + \sigma \mathbf{z} $$
where $\sigma$ is the step size (mutation strength), and $\mathbf{z} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I})$ is a vector of random Gaussian noise.

### Mutation and Selection Schemes

Offspring solutions are typically generated by adding Gaussian noise to the parent solutions. For a given parent $\mathbf{x}$, an offspring $\mathbf{x}'$ is created as $\mathbf{x}' = \mathbf{x} + \sigma \mathbf{z}$. After generating $\lambda$ offspring, their fitness values $f(\mathbf{x}')$ are evaluated. In a $(\mu, \lambda)$ strategy, the $\mu$ best offspring are selected to become the parents for the next generation. In a $(\mu + \lambda)$ strategy, the $\mu$ best individuals are selected from the combined pool of $\mu$ parents and $\lambda$ offspring.

### Step Size Adaptation

A crucial aspect of Evolution Strategies is the adaptation of the mutation strength, $\sigma$. An optimal $\sigma$ allows for efficient exploration and exploitation. Simple self-adaptation mechanisms involve encoding $\sigma$ as part of the individual's genome and allowing it to evolve. More advanced methods, like Covariance Matrix Adaptation Evolution Strategy (CMA-ES), adapt the full covariance matrix of the Gaussian mutation distribution, allowing the search to align with the local landscape of the objective function. The mutation step in CMA-ES is given by:
$$ \mathbf{x}' = \mathbf{x} + \sigma \mathbf{C}^{1/2} \mathbf{z} $$
Here, $\mathbf{C}$ is the covariance matrix, which is adapted over generations based on the success of previous mutations.

## Algorithm Steps

1. Initialize a population of $\mu$ parent solutions $\mathbf{x}_1, \dots, \mathbf{x}_\mu$, often randomly, and an initial mutation step size $\sigma$.
2. For each parent $\mathbf{x}_i$ in the current population, generate $\lambda/\mu$ offspring by adding Gaussian noise: $\mathbf{x}'_j = \mathbf{x}_i + \sigma \mathbf{z}_j$, where $\mathbf{z}_j \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I})$. This results in a total of $\lambda$ offspring.
3. Evaluate the fitness $f(\mathbf{x}'_j)$ for all $\lambda$ offspring.
4. Select the $\mu$ best individuals from the pool of offspring (for $(\mu, \lambda)$ strategy) or from the combined pool of parents and offspring (for $(\mu + \lambda)$ strategy) to form the new parent population.
5. Adapt the mutation step size $\sigma$ (and potentially the covariance matrix) based on the success of recent mutations.
6. Repeat steps 2-5 for a predefined number of generations or until a convergence criterion is met.

## Parameters

| Parameter | Type | Default | Range / Options | Description |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `population_size` | `int` | `50` | integer > 0 | The total number of offspring ($\lambda$) generated in each generation. |
| `parent_population_size` | `int` | `25` | integer > 0, parent_population_size <= population_size | The number of parent solutions ($\mu$) selected to propagate to the next generation. |
| `sigma` | `float` | `0.1` | float > 0 | The initial standard deviation of the Gaussian mutation noise, controlling the exploration step size. |
| `generations` | `int` | `1000` | integer > 0 | The total number of iterations or generations for which the optimization process will run. |
| `strategy` | `str` | `(mu, lambda)` | ['(mu, lambda)', '(mu + lambda)'] | The selection strategy used to form the next generation, either "(mu, lambda)" or "(mu + lambda)". |
| `seed` | `int` | `None` | integer >= 0 or None | Seed for the random number generator to ensure reproducibility. |

## Strengths

- Evolution Strategies are gradient-free, making them suitable for optimizing non-differentiable, discontinuous, or black-box objective functions.
- They are inherently parallelizable, as the evaluation of offspring fitness can be distributed across multiple computational units.
- ES can effectively escape local optima due to its stochastic nature and population-based search, leading to robust global optimization capabilities.
- The algorithm requires minimal assumptions about the objective function, making it highly versatile across various problem domains.
- ES can be more robust to noisy fitness evaluations compared to gradient-based methods.
- Basic Evolution Strategies are relatively simple to implement and understand.

## Weaknesses

- Evolution Strategies can be computationally expensive, especially for high-dimensional problems, due to the need to evaluate many candidate solutions per generation.
- Convergence can be slow compared to gradient-based methods when gradients are available and well-behaved.
- Performance is highly sensitive to hyperparameter tuning, such as population size and initial step size.
- Scaling to very high-dimensional problems (e.g., millions of parameters) without advanced variants like CMA-ES remains a challenge.
- Interpreting the search process and understanding why certain solutions are found can be difficult due to its black-box nature.

## Use Cases

### Robotics

Optimizing control policies for robots in complex environments where analytical gradients are difficult or impossible to obtain.

### Hyperparameter Optimization

Tuning the hyperparameters of machine learning models, which often involves non-differentiable validation metrics.

### Reinforcement Learning

Training agents for complex tasks by directly optimizing policy parameters, particularly in scenarios with high-dimensional state and action spaces.

### Black-box Optimization

Solving optimization problems where the objective function's internal workings are unknown or computationally expensive to differentiate.

### Engineering Design

Optimizing design parameters for structures, circuits, or aerodynamic shapes based on simulation results.

### Neural Architecture Search

Discovering optimal neural network architectures by treating architecture parameters as the search space.
