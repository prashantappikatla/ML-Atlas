## Overview

Autoregressive Integrated Moving Average (ARIMA) is a statistical model used for time series forecasting. It combines autoregressive (AR), differencing (I for integrated), and moving average (MA) components to capture various patterns in time-dependent data. ARIMA models are particularly effective for univariate time series that exhibit trends or seasonality, transforming non-stationary data into a stationary form for prediction.

## History

**1970** — [George Box](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_E._P._Box) and Gwilym Jenkins published "[Time Series Analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_series): Forecasting and Control," introducing the ARIMA model and a systematic methodology for its application, known as the Box-Jenkins method.

**1976** — The second edition of Box and Jenkins' seminal book further solidified ARIMA as a foundational method in time series analysis, detailing practical aspects of model identification, estimation, and diagnostic checking.

**1980** — ARIMA models became widely adopted across various fields, including economics, engineering, and business, due to their robust theoretical foundation and practical utility in forecasting.

## Mathematical Intuition

### Autoregressive (AR) Component

An autoregressive model of order $p$, denoted $AR(p)$, models the current value of a time series $y_t$ as a linear combination of its past $p$ values and a white noise error term $\epsilon_t$. This component captures the dependency between an observation and a number of lagged observations.
$$y_t = c + \phi_1 y_{t-1} + \phi_2 y_{t-2} + \dots + \phi_p y_{t-p} + \epsilon_t$$
where $c$ is a constant, $\phi_i$ are the autoregressive coefficients, and $\epsilon_t$ is white noise.

$$
y_t = c + \phi_1 y_{t-1} + \phi_2 y_{t-2} + \dots + \phi_p y_{t-p} + \epsilon_t
$$

### Integrated (I) Component

The "Integrated" part addresses non-stationarity in the time series by differencing. A time series is differenced $d$ times to achieve stationarity, meaning its statistical properties (mean, variance, autocorrelation) do not change over time. The differenced series $y'_t$ is defined as $y'_t = y_t - y_{t-1}$ for first-order differencing ($d=1$).
$$y'_t = (1-L)^d y_t$$
where $L$ is the lag operator, such that $L y_t = y_{t-1}$. The order $d$ represents the number of times the raw observations are differenced.

$$
y'_t = (1-L)^d y_t
$$

$$
L y_t = y_{t-1}
$$

### Moving Average (MA) Component

A moving average model of order $q$, denoted $MA(q)$, models the current value of a time series $y_t$ as a linear combination of past $q$ white noise error terms. This component captures the dependency between an observation and a residual error from a moving average model applied to lagged observations.
$$y_t = \mu + \epsilon_t + \theta_1 \epsilon_{t-1} + \theta_2 \epsilon_{t-2} + \dots + \theta_q \epsilon_{t-q}$$
where $\mu$ is the series mean, $\epsilon_t$ is white noise, and $\theta_i$ are the moving average coefficients.

$$
y_t = \mu + \epsilon_t + \theta_1 \epsilon_{t-1} + \theta_2 \epsilon_{t-2} + \dots + \theta_q \epsilon_{t-q}
$$

### Combining AR, I, MA

An ARIMA$(p, d, q)$ model combines these three components. The model is applied to the differenced series $y'_t = (1-L)^d y_t$. The full ARIMA model can be written as:
$$\left(1 - \sum_{i=1}^p \phi_i L^i\right) (1-L)^d y_t = \left(1 + \sum_{j=1}^q \theta_j L^j\right) \epsilon_t$$
This equation represents an $AR(p)$ model applied to the $d$-th differenced series, with the error terms modeled by an $MA(q)$ process.

$$
\left(1 - \sum_{i=1}^p \phi_i L^i\right) (1-L)^d y_t = \left(1 + \sum_{j=1}^q \theta_j L^j\right) \epsilon_t
$$

## Algorithm Steps

1. Model Identification: Analyze the time series using plots of the Autocorrelation Function (ACF) and Partial Autocorrelation Function (PACF) to determine appropriate orders $p$, $d$, and $q$.
2. Differencing for Stationarity: If the series is non-stationary (e.g., exhibits trend or seasonality), apply differencing $d$ times until the series becomes stationary, as indicated by constant mean and variance.
3. Parameter Estimation: Estimate the coefficients $\phi_i$ and $\theta_j$ for the chosen $p$ and $q$ orders using maximum likelihood estimation or non-linear least squares.
4. Diagnostic Checking: Evaluate the fitted model by examining the residuals; if residuals resemble white noise (no significant autocorrelation), the model is considered adequate, otherwise, refine the model parameters.
5. Forecasting: Use the fitted ARIMA model to generate future predictions for the time series, along with confidence intervals.

## Parameters

| Parameter | Type | Default | Range / Options | Description |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `order` | `tuple` | `(0, 0, 0)` | tuple of non-negative integers | The $(p, d, q)$ order of the model for the number of AR parameters, differences, and MA parameters, respectively. |
| `seasonal_order` | `tuple` | `(0, 0, 0, 0)` | tuple of non-negative integers | The $(P, D, Q, S)$ order of the seasonal component of the model for the seasonal AR parameters, seasonal differences, seasonal MA parameters, and periodicity, respectively. If non-zero, this effectively makes the model a SARIMA. |
| `trend` | `str or None` | `None` | ['n', 'c', 't', 'ct'] or None | Parameter controlling the deterministic trend polynomial. Options include 'n' (no trend), 'c' (constant), 't' (linear trend), 'ct' (constant and linear trend). |
| `enforce_stationarity` | `bool` | `True` | True, False | Whether to enforce stationarity of the AR parameters during estimation. |
| `enforce_invertibility` | `bool` | `True` | True, False | Whether to enforce invertibility of the MA parameters during estimation. |

## Strengths

- ARIMA models can effectively capture complex temporal dependencies, including trends and seasonality, through differencing.
- The Box-Jenkins methodology provides a systematic and well-established framework for model identification, estimation, and validation.
- Parameters of an ARIMA model are often interpretable, offering insights into the underlying dynamics of the time series.
- It is a robust and widely used method for short-to-medium term forecasting in various domains.
- ARIMA models can provide accurate forecasts for univariate time series with clear linear patterns.

## Weaknesses

- ARIMA models assume a linear relationship between observations and their lags, making them less suitable for highly non-linear time series.
- The model identification process (determining $p, d, q$) can be subjective and time-consuming, requiring expertise in time series analysis.
- They are sensitive to outliers and structural breaks in the time series, which can significantly impact model performance.
- ARIMA models are primarily designed for univariate time series and do not inherently handle multiple exogenous variables without extensions like ARIMAX.
- Forecasting accuracy tends to degrade significantly for longer prediction horizons.

## Use Cases

### Finance

Predicting stock prices, currency exchange rates, or commodity prices to inform trading strategies and risk management.

### Economics

Forecasting macroeconomic indicators such as GDP growth, inflation rates, and unemployment figures for policy formulation.

### Retail

Predicting future sales volumes for inventory management, supply chain optimization, and staffing decisions.

### Energy

Forecasting electricity demand or energy consumption patterns to optimize power generation and distribution.

### Meteorology

Short-term prediction of weather variables like temperature or rainfall for operational planning.

### Healthcare

Forecasting disease incidence or patient admissions to manage resource allocation and public health interventions.
